\begin{abstract}
Lists of the best footballers of a period are common, and they look precise.
We take one such list, a 48-player ranking produced by another AI model, and
ask a narrower question: how much of the answer can public match data decide,
and how much of what happens on the pitch do they not reach? We rebuild every
player estimate from raw data for matches played between 1 January 2020 and
31 August 2026: \NGames{} games and \NPlayersPanel{} players from
Transfermarkt-derived lineups, substitutions and events, with StatsBomb open
data for the tournaments that Transfermarkt lacks. Rankings are reported for a
pool of \NPool{} players fixed before any result: the 48 players of that list
and every player ranked in a Ballon d'Or from 2021 to 2026. Nine modules, with
eight hypotheses pre-registered before any player-level number existed,
measure sampling noise, the dependence between metrics, on-pitch impact by
regularised adjusted plus-minus, the share of that impact the individual
statistics cannot see, the spread over 5,760 definitions of ``best'', what the
Ballon d'Or vote rewards, big-game performance, and a synthesis that mixes
definitions, match-bootstrap replicates and draws of the unmeasured part. A
recovery study on the real schedule measures how often two of these estimators
recover a planted best player from data of this size.

The pre-registered analysis does not name a best player. Its most likely
number one among outfield players is \TopPlayerName{}, at \TopPlayerPFirst{}
(H2: \VerdictHTwo{}), and his lead rests on regularised adjusted plus-minus
(RAPM): box-score definitions of ``best'' rank other players first. Only
\SharePairsDecided{} of the pool's outfield pairwise orderings are decided at
the 0.9 level (H1: \VerdictHOne{}), almost none of them among the leaders.
Across the leading players, the definition of ``best'' moves ranks more than
sampling noise on average, narrowly: the mean ratio is \HFourRatio{} (H4:
\VerdictHFour{}) and the median \HFourMedianRatio{}. The decisive choice is
what a score is built from, not how seasons are combined (H5:
\VerdictHFive{}). The disattenuated share of on-pitch impact that the
statistics cannot see is \ShareHigh{} for centre-backs and defensive
midfielders; for attackers the correction over-corrects and the share is
clipped to \ShareLow{}, and both values depend on how RAPM's teammate leakage
is corrected. The 90\% interval of the difference is
[\ShareContrastParamLo, \ShareContrastParamHi] (H3: \VerdictHThree{}), and its
sign survives a post-hoc model in which quality clusters on teams. The
Ballon d'Or vote follows individual statistics more than team success in our
point estimates (H6: \VerdictHSix{}), but the order reverses in
\ShapleyTeamAboveStatsBoot{} of bootstrap replicates
(\CuratedTeamAboveStatsBoot{} with curated team honours) and under one reading
of the statistics. Knockout over-performance is too noisy for its persistence
test to refute anything (H7: \VerdictHSeven{}), although the observed
persistence is positive, and H8 is not tested, because the coarse shot data it
needed were withdrawn over their terms of use.

After these results we found a flaw in the pre-registered analysis: nothing in
it adjusts for league strength. Its RAPM gives the players of the three
leading Portuguese clubs an average close to that of Real Madrid's players, and
\QuinonesSpecShare{} of its definitions rank first a forward with two
qualifying seasons, both in the Saudi league. A post-hoc analysis, labelled as
such throughout, adds league means to RAPM and league factors to box-score
counts, both estimated from our data. Under it the most likely number one is
\LeagueTopName{}, at \LeagueTopPFirst{}; \TopPlayerName{} is second, and
\LeagueSharePairsDecided{} of pairs are decided. Adding the uncertainty of how
RAPM divides goals among teammates, which neither analysis carries by default,
lowers the probabilities of the players who lead on RAPM. The hypotheses stay
decided by the pre-registered analysis. What holds in every variant is the
paper's main result: no player has a probability above
\AllVariantsMaxPFirst{} of being the best, and at most \AllVariantsMaxDecided{}
of pairwise orderings are decided. In simulated leagues on the real schedule,
pooled RAPM ranks a planted best player first in \RecoveryRapmFirst{} of
leagues and a box-score composite in \RecoveryBoxFirst{}. A ranking of the best
player of the 2020s is a statement about the definition chosen more than about
the players.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The motivating object of this paper is a list. It ranks 48 footballers of the
2020s, each with a score out of 100, and it was produced by another AI model
at the author's request. We call it V1. The model built V1 by mixing human
ratings, team results and guesses about longevity with weights it set by
hand, and it conceded, when asked, that the score counts some qualities twice.
Its inputs cannot be recovered, so its scores cannot be recomputed. V1 is not
unusual. It is a typical ``best player'' ranking: one number per player,
ordered, with no statement of how sure it is.

A list of this kind hides at least four things. First, a season is a short
sample. A player's per-90 rate over one season carries sampling noise that
a list does not show. Second, ``best'' has no single definition. Peak season
or sustained output, rate or volume, goals or on-pitch impact, league or
Champions League: each choice is defensible, and each can reorder the top.
Third, public statistics do not reach every part of the game. Off-ball
movement, positional defending, pressing and leadership leave little or no
trace in a box score. Fourth, recognition is not quality. The Ballon d'Or
measures what voters reward, which can follow trophies and reputation.

This paper does not tune V1's weights. It asks:

\begin{quote}
\emph{How much of ``who was the best footballer of 2020--2026'' can public
statistics decide, and how much of on-pitch reality can they not reach?}
\end{quote}

The answer is a probability and a variance account, not a list. For each
player in a fixed pool we report the probability of being ranked first, rank
intervals, and the share of pairwise comparisons that the data decide. We
split each player's rank variance into named sources: the definition of
``best'', sampling noise, and the part of on-pitch impact the statistics
cannot see. We treat the estimand, what ``best'' means, as a source of
uncertainty to be measured rather than a choice to be hidden.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{A variance accounting of ``who is best''.} A mixture over every
    definition in a full factorial, every match-bootstrap replicate and draws
    of the unmeasured component, with an exact nested decomposition of each
    player's rank variance into specification, sampling and unmeasured terms
    (M8).
  \item \emph{A measured unmeasured share.} The share of on-pitch impact,
    estimated by regularised adjusted plus-minus (RAPM), that individual
    statistics cannot predict, disattenuated for the noise in both, per
    position group (M4). Two corrections, fixed before any real-data run of
    M4, make it usable: a cross-half estimator that removes shared match noise, and an
    attribution factor that removes the teammate leakage which split-half
    reliability counts as signal.
  \item \emph{A complete multiverse of definitions.} 5,760 specifications,
    with exact first- and total-order variance indices of each player's rank
    for each choice (M5).
  \item \emph{A decomposition of recognition.} What the Ballon d'Or vote
    rewards, split by exact Shapley values into individual statistics, impact,
    team success and reputation (M6).
  \item \emph{A recovery study.} Simulations on the real schedule that show
    how often two of these estimators, pooled RAPM and a box-score composite,
    recover a planted best player from data of this size (M9).
  \item \emph{A league-strength adjustment, post hoc.} League means inside
    RAPM and league factors for box-score counts, both estimated from the data
    and validated on synthetic leagues, added after the pre-registered results
    showed that nothing in them adjusts for league strength
    (Section~\ref{sec:league}).
  \item \emph{A pre-registered, reproducible pipeline.} Hypotheses and
    amendments were committed before the results they concern, except
    A16 (ii), A16 (iii) and A17 to A23, which were recorded after the analyses
    they concern and are labelled so. Every number in this paper is written by
    a script from a results file; none is typed.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
Eight hypotheses were pre-registered (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).
H1: fewer than half of the pool's outfield pairwise orderings are decided.
H2: no outfield player has a probability of at least 0.5 of being ranked first.
H3: the unmeasured share of on-pitch impact is larger for centre-backs and
defensive midfielders than for centre-forwards and wingers.
H4: for the leading players, the definition of ``best'' moves ranks more than
sampling noise does.
H5: among all modelling choices, how seasons are aggregated over time moves
the rank of the most likely number one the most.
H6: the Ballon d'Or rewards team success more than individual statistics.
H7: ``big-game player'' is not a stable trait.
H8: coarse shot data lose little against full event data at season level;
it is not tested (A6, A11, A15).

\paragraph{Two analyses.}
The paper reports two analyses and keeps them apart. The first is the
pre-registered one (Section~\ref{sec:results}); it decides the hypotheses.
After its results we found that it has a specification flaw: it does not adjust
for league strength. The second is a post-hoc analysis that adds that
adjustment (Section~\ref{sec:league}). Its method was fixed before any
league-adjusted ranking existed, but after the pre-registered results were
known, so it is exploratory. Where the two give different answers, we report
both and say which is which.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Randomness in football.}
Goals are rare, and chance shapes many of them. Coding Premier League goals
for six markers of chance (deflections, rebounds, post hits, long shots,
goalkeeper or defender involvement), \citet{wunderlich2021randomness} found
that about 46\% of goals involve at least one, a share that fell over the
seasons they studied; \citet{lames2018chance} introduced the approach.
Bundesliga goals behave approximately as independent Poisson events given
pre-match rates \citep{heuer2010poisson}. A match is a weak experiment for
picking the better team \citep{skinner2009experiments}, luck is substantial
even in competitive leagues \citep{aoki2017luck}, and short-run results can
misstate underlying performance \citep{brechot2020randomness}. If team results
are this noisy, player-level summaries built on them are noisier.

\paragraph{Reliability and measurement error.}
\citet{franks2016metaanalytics} proposed meta-metrics for sports statistics:
stability, discrimination and independence. Our M1 and M2 make the first and
third operational. The step-up of reliability for a longer test is due to
\citet{spearman1910faulty} and \citet{brown1910experimental}, and the
correction of a correlation for unreliability to \citet{spearman1904proof}.
Disattenuated correlations can exceed one and have awkward sampling
behaviour \citep{charles2005attenuation}, which is why we clip and bootstrap
them. \citet{lames2007reliable} argued that performance indicators in game
sports are inherently unstable because performance emerges from interaction
with the opponent. Physical outputs illustrate the point: match-to-match
coefficients of variation of about 16\% for high-speed running and 31\% for
sprint distance \citep{gregson2010variability}. We use the bootstrap
\citep{efron1979bootstrap}, empirical-Bayes shrinkage \citep{efron1975stein}
and the intraclass correlation \citep{shrout1979icc}.

\paragraph{Plus-minus ratings.}
Adjusted plus-minus regresses a score margin on who is on the pitch.
Ridge regularisation \citep{hoerl1970ridge} made it usable in basketball
\citep{sill2010rapm} and hockey \citep{macdonald2012ridge}, after regression
APM \citep{macdonald2011apm}, where collinear lineups otherwise make error bars
wide. With careful
shrinkage, most hockey players are not measurably different from zero
\citep{gramacy2013hockey}. In football, plus-minus ratings were introduced by
\citet{saebo2015transfer} and used to value transfers
\citep{saebo2019financial}. They have been weighted by opponent strength and
goal importance \citep{schultze2018weighted}, split into offence and defence
\citep{hvattum2020offdef}, and given expected-goals targets because the goal
target is sparse \citep{kharrat2020plusminus}. \citet{hvattum2019review}
reviews the field. Top-down plus-minus was more reliable across data sets
than bottom-up action values \citep{hvattum2021bottomup}, and Elo team ratings
and plus-minus player ratings carry complementary forecasting information
\citep{arntzen2021ratings}. The closest prior to our M4 is
\citet{gelade2020events}: according to its abstract, event-level statistics
explain 22\% to 38\% of the variance in football plus-minus ratings, depending
on position. The abstract reports no correction for the noise in either side.

\paragraph{Valuing events and what lies off the ball.}
Action-value models credit every on-ball action with its effect on short-run
scoring chances: VAEP \citep{decroos2019vaep}, expected threat (xT), a Markov
grid described in a blog post \citep{singh2019xt}, and expected possession
value from tracking data \citep{fernandez2019epv,fernandez2021epv}. xT and VAEP
rank players differently, and xT cannot value defensive actions
\citep{vanroy2020xtvaep}. Defensive actions can be valued by the threat they
stop \citep{merhej2021defensive}. Off-ball value needs tracking data: pitch
control and off-ball scoring opportunity \citep{spearman2018beyond}, space
creation \citep{fernandez2018wide} and pass options \citep{power2017passes};
counterpressing is detected from combined positional and event data
\citep{bauer2021counterpressing}. Role-aware ratings
\citep{pappalardo2019playerank} and role detection from events or tracking
\citep{aalbers2019roles,bialkowski2014roles} show that nominal positions are a
coarse summary.

\paragraph{Expected goals.}
Shot-quality models go back at least to \citet{pollard1997strategies}.
Location is among the strongest xG features \citep{robberechts2020xg}, and
tracking data improve accuracy \citep{anzer2021xg}. Shot-location xG tracks
mid-table teams well, while top teams over-achieve it and bottom teams
under-achieve it \citep{rathke2017xg}. Player-ability, team-ability
and psychological features add information to xG, and xG predicts future team
success better than traditional statistics \citep{mead2023xg}. Player finishing effects
persist in a Bayesian hierarchical model \citep{scholtes2024bayesxg}, while
``goals minus xG'' is a poor finishing measure on small samples
\citep[preprint;][]{davis2024finishing}.

\paragraph{Uncertainty in rankings.}
League tables of institutions carry large model-based uncertainty and should
be reported with intervals \citep{goldstein1996league}. The n-out-of-n
bootstrap of an empirical ranking can be inconsistent when true values are
close \citep{hall2009bootstrap}. \citet{mogstad2024ranks} give marginal and
simultaneous confidence sets for ranks under weak assumptions. Agreement
between rankings is measured by Kendall's $W$ \citep{kendall1939rankings}.

\paragraph{Multiverse and sensitivity analysis.}
A multiverse analysis runs every reasonable analysis path
\citep{steegen2016multiverse}, and a specification curve plots them
\citep{simonsohn2020specification}. When 29 teams analysed one football data
set on referees' red cards, their odds ratios ranged from 0.89 to 2.93, and 20
of them reported a significant effect \citep{silberzahn2018many}. In a larger
many-analysts study, over 95\% of the variance in results was unexplained by
coded analytic decisions \citep{breznau2022many}. Variance-based sensitivity
indices \citep{sobol2001sensitivity,homma1996importance,saltelli2002sensitivity,saltelli2010total,saltelli2008primer}
attribute an output's variance to its inputs; we compute them exactly on a
full factorial. For composite indicators, \citet{saisana2005composite} applied
such indices to a country ranking, and \citet{paruolo2013voodoo} showed that a
variable's nominal weight rarely matches its actual importance when the
components are correlated, which is the formal version of V1's double-counting
problem. The OECD handbook covers construction \citep{oecd2008composite}.

\paragraph{Recognition.}
Voters for the FIFA Ballon d'Or (national-team coaches, captains and
journalists) favour their own country, league and continent, but the effect on
totals is small \citep{coupe2018ballondor}. In the Best FIFA Men's Player vote,
voting bias rises with cultural similarity, and media voters are the least
biased \citep{johnson2022voting}. Votes have concentrated over time on certain kinds
of player \citep{anderson2020celebrity}. An undergraduate study of the 2025
edition found that trophies improve a statistical model of the vote
\citep{olmeta2026ballondor}. Market values, often used as reputation, are crowd
estimates \citep{herm2014crowd} that can be matched by data-driven estimates
\citep{muller2017market}; squad values forecast national-team results
\citep{peeters2018wisdom}, and values rise with both talent and popularity
\citep{franck2012superstar}. We model the vote with Plackett--Luce
\citep{plackett1975permutations,luce1959choice}, fitted by maximum likelihood
\citep{hunter2004mm}, and split explained variance by Shapley values
\citep{shapley1953value}, the LMG decomposition
\citep{lindeman1980multivariate,gromping2007relimp,gromping2006relaimpo}, which
is related to dominance analysis \citep{budescu1993dominance}.

\paragraph{Clutch and the hot hand.}
Perceived streaks in basketball shooting were judged consistent with chance
by the test of \citet{gilovich1985hothand}; \citet{miller2018hothand} showed
that this test is biased toward ``no hot hand'' and corrected it. A clutch trait is detectable
in tennis, which has many high-stakes points per player
\citep{gonzalezdiaz2012tennis}. In football, the only player-level study we
found models mental pressure and reports differences between players, without
a test of stability over time \citep{bransen2019choke}. Penalty shoot-outs
show avoidance under pressure \citep{jordet2008avoidance}; a reported
first-kicker advantage \citep{apesteguia2010pressure} did not replicate in a
larger sample \citep{kocher2012pressure}.

\paragraph{Context.}
Shared success between teammates predicts wins \citep{mukherjee2019shared},
but at match level shared experience has no independent effect once team
strength is controlled \citep{lecoz2025shared}. Managers differ in the points
they gain \citep{muehlheusser2018managers}. Possession depends on location,
opponent and score \citep{lago2009possession}, so event counts partly measure
opportunity. Home advantage fell in the German top-division ghost games of
2019/20 \citep{fischer2021ghost}, and empty stadiums narrowed the home--away
gap in cards \citep{bryson2021crowd}. Elo ratings

\section{What numbers cannot reach}

The evidence is solid in a few places: randomness in goals, the match-to-match
noise of physical outputs, the limits of plus-minus precision, similarity bias
in award voting, and the gap between weight and importance in composite
indices \citep{paruolo2013voodoo}. It is thin where the remainder is likely to
be largest. Tracking studies show that off-ball movement and defensive
positioning exist and can be measured, but none gives a population-level share
of player impact. Aerial play, goalkeeping beyond xG, leadership, mentality and
league-strength adjustment have no verified size at all. The closest
published quantity to what M4 estimates is the 22--38\% of plus-minus variance
that event statistics explain, by the abstract of \citet{gelade2020events},
which reports no correction for noise. M4 differs in two ways: our statistics
are box scores, not event data, and our estimate is corrected for the noise in
both sides.

\section{Data}

\subsection{Sources}

\paragraph{Transfermarkt datasets.}
Games, appearances, lineups (positions and captaincy), game events (goals and
assists with their minute, substitutions with their minute, cards), player
profiles, market valuations, club games and competitions come from the
transfermarkt-datasets project \citep{transfermarktdatasets}, a public CSV
mirror of data originating from transfermarkt.com, snapshot of 2026-09-05.
Its identifiers are the keys of the whole study: \texttt{game\_id} for games and
\texttt{player\_id} for players.

\paragraph{StatsBomb open data.}
Full event data with locations and StatsBomb xG \citep{statsbombopendata}. In
the window, the men's tournaments are complete: the 2022 World Cup, Euro 2020,
Euro 2024, the 2024 Copa Am\'erica and the 2023 Africa Cup of Nations. The club
seasons are single-team samples: Barcelona in La Liga 2019/20 and 2020/21, PSG
in Ligue 1 2021/22 and 2022/23, Leverkusen in the Bundesliga 2023/24, and a few
Inter Miami matches in MLS 2023. Any StatsBomb-based estimate is therefore a
tournament-plus-three-clubs sample, not a league sample. Of \NSbMatches{}
StatsBomb matches in the window, \NSbMatchesLinked{} are linked to a
Transfermarkt game, and \SbPlayerLinkRate{} of the StatsBomb player identities
in linked matches are linked to a Transfermarkt player.

\paragraph{Ballon d'Or.}
The points of every ranked player in the 2019 and 2021--2026 editions were
compiled by hand from at least two independent outlets per edition, with each
row's sources recorded. Official pages could not be fetched. Each edition's
points sum exactly to the number of voters times the points per ballot, which
checks that no total is missing or mistyped. The 2020 edition was cancelled.
The 2019 edition is used only as the previous edition for 2021 (A3). The
editions of 2021 to 2023 used five names per ballot, those from 2024 ten.
The 2021 edition voted on the calendar year; from 2022 on, the vote covers a
club season.

\paragraph{Sources not used.}
Understat, FBref and the ClubElo interface refused scripted access when probed,
and we did not work around a refusal. Team strength is therefore computed
in-house (Section~\ref{sec:elo}). ESPN's public match data were considered as
a third source and withdrawn over their terms of use before any analysis used
them (A15). A public, openly licensed set of Wyscout match events
\citep{pappalardo2019dataset} is another event-data source; it was not used.

\subsection{Window and seasons}

The study window is matches dated 2020-01-01 to 2026-08-31 inclusive. The end
date is the Transfermarkt snapshot; matches from September to November 2026 are
outside the window. Club competitions keep Transfermarkt's own season label,
which handles the extended 2019/20 season and calendar-year leagues. A
national-team tournament or the Club World Cup takes the season label of its
first match, so a summer tournament belongs to the season that has just ended:
Euro 2020 to 2020/21, the 2022 World Cup to 2022/23, the 2026 World Cup to
2025/26.

\subsection{Stints and the panel}

A \emph{stint} is a maximal interval of a game with no substitution and no red
card. Minutes are integers, with stoppage time folded into minutes 45 and 90
(Transfermarkt's convention), so stint boundaries are the distinct change
minutes plus kick-off and the final minute (90, or 120 after extra time). A
change at minute $m$ takes effect at the start of the stint that begins at $m$.
A goal at minute $m$ belongs to the stint $(s, t]$ with $s < m \le t$, so a goal
at the minute of a change goes to the stint that ends there. Each stint
records the players on the pitch for both sides and the goals each side
scored in it.

Transfermarkt has events but no lineups for the 2022 World Cup and Euros 2020
and 2024. For these \NSbStintGames{} games, stints and appearances come from
StatsBomb's starting elevens, substitutions, sendings-off and goals, mapped to
Transfermarkt's conventions. StatsBomb games and players are linked to
Transfermarkt identifiers: games by date (within one day) and team-name
similarity; players first by an optimal one-to-one assignment within each
linked game's two sides, then, for those still unlinked, within the same club
or national team, then within the same citizenship, each step with a minimum
similarity and a minimum lead over the runner-up. Ambiguous and conflicting
links are reported, not hidden.

The panel has \NGames{} games, of which \NGamesWithStints{} have stints, and
\NPlayerMatches{} player-matches with at least one minute, by \NPlayersPanel{}
players. A player-match row holds minutes, goals, non-penalty goals, penalty
goals, assists, cards, whether he started, his lineup position, and the goals
for and against his side while he was on the pitch. Competitions are grouped
into four scopes: \emph{league} (first-tier domestic leagues), \emph{ucl}
(the Champions League proper), \emph{intl} (national-team competitions) and
\emph{other} (cups, the Europa and Conference Leagues, the Club World Cup and
qualifying rounds). \NPlayerSeasonsQualifying{} player-seasons reach the
pre-registered floor of 900 minutes.

Each player has one position group over the window, his minutes-weighted modal
lineup position mapped to eight groups: goalkeeper (GK), centre-back (CB),
full-back (FB), defensive midfielder (DM), central midfielder (CM), attacking
midfielder (AM), winger or wide midfielder (W), and centre-forward or second
striker (CF).

\subsection{Team strength}
\label{sec:elo}

Team strength is an Elo rating \citep{elo1978rating} with a goal-difference
multiplier and home advantage, in the form used for world football and as a
covariate by \citet{hvattum2010elo}. Before game $m$, with ratings $R_{\mathrm H}$
and $R_{\mathrm A}$ and home advantage $H$,
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm H} = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{-(R_{\mathrm H} + H - R_{\mathrm A})/400}},
  \qquad
  \Delta R = K\, G\, (S_{\mathrm H} - E_{\mathrm H}),
  \label{eq:elo}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\mathrm H}$ is 1, $\tfrac12$ or 0 for a home win, draw or loss,
$G = 1$ for a goal difference $|\mathit{gd}| \le 1$, $1.5$ for $|\mathit{gd}| = 2$
and $(11 + |\mathit{gd}|)/8$ otherwise, and the home rating gains $\Delta R$
while the away rating loses it. Ratings run over every Transfermarkt game from
2015-07-01, so the years before the window are a burn-in. Clubs and national
teams are separate pools. In each, $K$ and $H$ minimise the one-step-ahead squared error of
$E_{\mathrm H}$ against $S_{\mathrm H}$ on games before 2020 and are then
frozen: $K = \EloKClub$ and $H = \EloHClub$ for clubs, $K = \EloKNational$ and
$H = \EloHNational$ for national teams. The source has no neutral-venue flag,
so $H$ also applies to tournament games at neutral venues. Elo enters M2
(partial correlations), M5 (the context dimension) and a robustness fit of M3;
it is not a covariate in the primary M3 fit (A12).

This Elo compares teams within a league. It does not compare leagues, a fact
we established only after the pre-registered results
(Section~\ref{sec:league}). The design assumed that games between clubs of
different leagues would tie the league levels together. They do not. In
several leagues, more rating points come in from domestic-cup games against
clubs of lower divisions, which are in no covered league, enter at 1,500 and
lose, than from continental games; leagues whose cups are in the data are
inflated and the others are not. The leagues that enter the snapshot in
2024/25 start at 1,500 and stay near it, and most leagues covered throughout
drift upward with time in the data. The Danish league's mean rating over the
window is above those of Serie A and Ligue 1,
although Danish clubs lose on average against clubs of other covered leagues
and French clubs win. Elo systems built to compare leagues let a league's
level move only through games between leagues \citep{csato2024elo}, or
exclude lower tiers and renormalise each season
\citep{shelopugin2023leagues}; ours does neither.

\subsection{The player pool}

The pool was fixed before any result. It is the union of the 48 players of V1
and every player ranked in a Ballon d'Or edition from 2021 to 2026 (the 2026
edition qualifies under A2). It holds \NPool{} players: \NPoolOutfield{}
outfield players and \NPoolGoalkeepers{} goalkeepers, ranked separately.
\NPoolBdoOnly{} are not on V1. Names were mapped to Transfermarkt identifiers

for V1-only players. \NPoolMediumConfidence{} mappings have medium confidence
and \NPoolUnmapped{} names are unmapped. Appendix~\ref{app:pool} lists the
pool. Estimates are produced for every player in the data, because RAPM needs
them all; rankings are reported for the pool only.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

Every module is a pure function of its inputs: no input or output, and
randomness only through an explicit random-number generator seeded from the
master seed 20261114 (A12). Every estimator is unit-tested on synthetic data
with a planted truth. Throughout, $i$ and $j$ index players, $m$ matches, $q$
seasons and $g$ position groups. $d_{im}$ is the minutes player $i$ played in
match $m$ and $c_{im}$ a count (goals, assists, cards). A per-90 rate over a set
of matches $\mathcal M$ is minutes-weighted, never a mean of per-match rates:
\begin{equation}
  x_i(\mathcal M) = 90\, \frac{\sum_{m \in \mathcal M} c_{im}}{\sum_{m \in \mathcal M} d_{im}}.
  \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{M1: sampling noise}
\label{sec:m1}

M1 asks how much of a player's per-90 number is the player and how much the
luck of which matches he played. The unit is a player-season $(i, q)$ with at
least 900 minutes.

\paragraph{Split-half reliability.}
In each of $S = 200$ random splits, a unit's matches are put in random order
and dealt alternately into halves $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal B$, whose match
counts differ by at most one. A unit enters split $s$ if both halves reach 450
minutes. Let $r_s$ be the Pearson correlation, across entering units, of
$x_{iq}(\mathcal A)$ and $x_{iq}(\mathcal B)$. A half is half as long as the
season, so $r_s$ is stepped up with the Spearman--Brown formula
\citep{spearman1910faulty,brown1910experimental}, which for a test lengthened
$L$ times reads $\rho_L = L\rho / (1 + (L - 1)\rho)$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat\rho = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} \frac{2 r_s}{1 + r_s}.
  \label{eq:sb}
\end{equation}
The standard deviation over splits measures the split-to-split spread. Units
differ in minutes, so $\hat\rho$ is the reliability at about the harmonic mean
of their minutes.

\paragraph{Stabilisation.}
If a unit's observed rate is a true rate $\theta$ plus noise $e$ with
$\Var(e) = \phi / d$ after $d$ minutes, and $\Var(\theta) = \psi$, the
reliability after $d$ minutes is
\begin{equation}
  \rho(d) = \frac{\psi}{\psi + \phi/d} = \frac{d}{d + k}, \qquad k = \phi/\psi,
  \label{eq:stab}
\end{equation}
which is the Spearman--Brown curve in minutes. The stabilisation constant $k$
is the number of minutes at which noise variance equals signal variance. To
estimate it, each half of a random split is cut to the shortest prefix that
reaches a level $\ell \in \{180, 360, 540, 720, 900\}$ minutes, and the raw
(not stepped-up) half-correlation $\bar r_\ell$ is averaged over 200 splits.
Its abscissa $d_\ell$ is the harmonic mean of the actual half minutes, and
$n_\ell$ is the mean number of entering units. Then
\begin{equation}
  \hat k = \argmin_{k > 0} \sum_\ell n_\ell \left(\bar r_\ell - \frac{d_\ell}{d_\ell + k}\right)^2 .
  \label{eq:kfit}
\end{equation}
Solving $\rho(d) = \rho^\ast$ gives $d = k\rho^\ast/(1 - \rho^\ast)$: reliability
reaches 0.5 at $k$ minutes and 0.7 at $7k/3$. Only units whose halves reach
$\ell$ enter level $\ell$, so the population shifts toward regulars as $\ell$
grows; the counts are reported.

\paragraph{Bootstrap.}
Each player metric gets a 90\% percentile interval from 200 bootstrap
replicates \citep{efron1979bootstrap} that resample matches with replacement
within the unit.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{M2: structure and double counting}
\label{sec:m2}

M2 is exploratory. It asks how many independent things the metrics measure,
and how much a weighted composite counts twice. Its input is the outfield
player-seasons of at least 900 minutes over all competitions, with seven per-90
metrics available for every match (A11): goals, non-penalty goals, penalty
goals, assists, yellow cards, red cards and raw on-pitch goal difference. Each
player-season also carries its team Elo (the minutes-weighted mean of his
club's pre-match Elo), its league-season (his main domestic league) and its
main club. Metrics are standardised, so the analysis works on the correlation
matrix $R$. Goals are exactly non-penalty goals plus penalty goals, so the
eigenvalue analyses below use the six other metrics; correlations, clustering,
the team share and the composite keep all seven.

\paragraph{Correlation and partial correlation.}
Spearman correlations describe raw dependence. Partial correlations control
team strength and context: each metric $x_k$ is replaced by its OLS residual
$e_k = (I - P_W)\, x_k$ on a design $W$ with an intercept, team Elo (linear) and
one indicator per league-season (fixed effects), and
\begin{equation}
  \rho^{\text{partial}}_{kl} = \corr(e_k, e_l).
  \label{eq:partial}
\end{equation}
The Spearman variant is the Spearman correlation of the same residuals.
Metrics are clustered by average linkage on the distance $1 - |\rho_{kl}|$,
which treats a strong negative correlation as redundancy too. Distance
correlation \citep{szekely2007dcor}, zero only under independence, detects
non-linear dependence, with a permutation test.

\paragraph{Dimension.}
Let $\nu_1 \ge \dots \ge \nu_p \ge 0$ be the eigenvalues of $R$ (rounding
negatives clipped to zero), so $\sum_j \nu_j = p$. A permutation version of
Horn's parallel analysis \citep{horn1965parallel} compares each $\nu_j$ with the eigenvalues of the
correlation matrix of data whose columns have been permuted independently,
which keeps each metric's skewed marginal and destroys the dependence. With
$\nu^{(r)}_j$ from permutation $r = 1, \dots, 200$, component $j$ is retained
while
\begin{equation}
  \nu_j > Q_{0.95}\big(\nu^{(1)}_j, \dots, \nu^{(200)}_j\big),
  \label{eq:parallel}
\end{equation}
stopping at the first $j$ that fails. Two continuous measures of the effective
number of independent metrics are reported: the participation ratio
\citep[preprint;][]{gao2017dimensionality} and the entropy effective number
\citep{roy2007effective},
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PR} = \frac{\left(\sum_j \nu_j\right)^2}{\sum_j \nu_j^2},
  \qquad
  N_H = \exp\Big(-\sum_j \pi_j \log \pi_j\Big),\quad \pi_j = \frac{\nu_j}{\sum_l \nu_l}.
  \label{eq:pr}
\end{equation}
PR equals $p$ for independent metrics and 1 for one metric repeated $p$ times.
Both count the noise floor, so $k$ clean factors give slightly more than $k$.
Maximum-likelihood factor analysis \citep{joreskog1967ml} with varimax
rotation gives the loadings.

\paragraph{Team share.}
The team intraclass correlation ICC(1) \citep{shrout1979icc} is the share of a
metric's within-league-season variance that lies between clubs. With club
$\times$ league-season cells nested in league-season strata, $N$ rows, $G$
cells, $S'$ strata and cell sizes $n_c$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ICC} = \frac{\mathrm{MS}_B - \mathrm{MS}_W}{\mathrm{MS}_B + (n_0 - 1)\mathrm{MS}_W},
  \qquad
  n_0 = \frac{N - \sum_c n_c^2 / N_{\text{stratum}(c)}}{G - S'},
  \label{eq:icc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{MS}_B$ is the mean square of cell means around their stratum
mean and $\mathrm{MS}_W$ the within-cell mean square. It is an upper bound on
the team's causal share, because clubs that recruit similar players raise it
too. The interval is the standard bootstrap interval, the estimate $\pm 1.645$
bootstrap standard deviations over 1,000 resamples of clubs (A17). This
replaced the percentile interval after M2 had run, because on synthetic panels
the club bootstrap sits below an about unbiased estimate; on the real data that
offset is larger, so the intervals are approximate.

\paragraph{Double counting in a composite.}
A composite $C = \sum_k w_k z_k$ of z-scored metrics has $\Var(C) = w^\top R w$
and $\Cov(z_k, C) = (Rw)_k$. Its \emph{effective} shares and the shares its
weights \emph{claim} under independence are
\begin{equation}
  e_k = \frac{w_k (Rw)_k}{w^\top R w}, \qquad c_k = \frac{w_k^2}{w^\top w},
  \qquad \text{inflation} = \frac{w^\top R w}{w^\top w},
  \qquad \text{PC1 share} = \frac{\nu_1 (v_1^\top w)^2}{w^\top R w},
  \label{eq:composite}
\end{equation}
with $v_1$ the first eigenvector of $R$. With $R = I$, $e_k = c_k$ and the
inflation is 1. A metric entered twice under two names (correlation 1) raises
the inflation and its effective share above what its weights claim; this is
the mechanism of \citet{paruolo2013voodoo}. We apply the diagnostics to a
V1-like composite. A13 (i) gives its weights: goals 0.30, non-penalty goals
0.15, assists 0.25, on-pitch goal difference 0.20, RAPM offence 0.05 and RAPM
defence 0.05. M2's metrics do not include RAPM, so M2 uses the four other
weights, renormalised to sum to one; the diagnostics do not depend on the scale
of $w$. These weights are a stylised imitation of V1's emphasis, not V1's
weights, which cannot be recovered.

\subsection{M3: on-pitch impact}
\label{sec:m3}

M3 estimates each player's effect on goals while he is on the pitch by
regularised adjusted plus-minus (RAPM) on stints, with separate offensive and
defensive effects \citep{hvattum2020offdef,sill2010rapm,macdonald2012ridge}.

\paragraph{Model.}
Each stint $\ell$, of $d_\ell$ minutes and competition-season $\kappa(\ell)$,
gives two rows, one per attacking side $a \in \{\mathrm H, \mathrm A\}$. Let
$\bar a$ be the other side, $\mathcal L_{\ell a}$ the players of side $a$ on the
pitch and $g_{\ell a}$ the goals side $a$ scored in the stint. The response is
goals per 90 and its expectation is
\begin{equation}
  y_{\ell a} = \frac{90\, g_{\ell a}}{d_\ell},
  \qquad
  \E[y_{\ell a}] = \sum_{i \in \mathcal L_{\ell a}} o_i
    - \sum_{j \in \mathcal L_{\ell \bar a}} \delta_j
    + h_{\kappa(\ell)}\, \mathbb 1[a = \mathrm H] + \mu_{\kappa(\ell)} .
  \label{eq:rapm-model}
\end{equation}
$o_i$ is player $i$'s offensive effect (goals scored per 90) and $\delta_i$ his
defensive effect, signed as goals prevented per 90, so higher is better for
both. His total effect is $\beta_i = o_i + \delta_i$. $h_\kappa$ is the home
advantage and $\mu_\kappa$ the intercept of competition-season $\kappa$; both
are per competition-season (A8), because home advantage fell in German
top-division ghost games of 2019/20 \citep{fischer2021ghost} and empty stadiums
narrowed the home--away gap in cards \citep{bryson2021crowd}. A
player gets his own pair of columns if he played at least 900 minutes in the
window; all others share one replacement pair, and two such players on the
pitch count twice. Let $\mathcal P$ be the set of column pairs. The estimates
solve the weighted ridge problem \citep{hoerl1970ridge}
\begin{equation}
  \min_{o, \delta, h, \mu}\;
  \sum_{\ell, a} d_\ell \big(y_{\ell a} - \E[y_{\ell a}]\big)^2
  + \lambda \sum_{i \in \mathcal P} \big(o_i^2 + \delta_i^2\big),
  \label{eq:rapm-obj}
\end{equation}
with home and intercept terms unpenalised. The penalty resolves the
confounding between the sum of the eleven player effects and the intercepts by
centring players near zero. Each fit is one call to LSQR
\citep{paige1982lsqr} on the column-scaled system. $\lambda$ minimises the
minutes-weighted squared error of the per-90 response in 5-fold
cross-validation with folds of whole games (A12).

\paragraph{No Elo covariate.}
The primary fit has no team-strength covariate (A12): the players on the pitch
are the team's strength, so adjusting for Elo would remove part of the signal
being estimated. A robustness fit adds $+\gamma\,(R_{\mathrm H} - R_{\mathrm A})$
to the home-attacking row and $-\gamma\,(R_{\mathrm H} - R_{\mathrm A})$ to the
away-attacking row, with $\gamma$ unpenalised.

\paragraph{Uncertainty and reliability.}
Standard errors come from $B = 200$ bootstrap replicates that resample whole
games \citep{efron1979bootstrap}; each replicate is also fitted on two random
halves of its games, so that M4 can re-estimate reliabilities inside it.
Split-half reliability $\rho^{\mathrm{SH}}_g$ fits the model on two random
halves of the games at the same $\lambda$, correlates the two estimates across
the players of group $g$ with own columns and at least 450 minutes in each
half, averages over 20 random game splits and steps up with
Equation~\eqref{eq:sb}.

\paragraph{Attribution factor.}
Split-half reliability measures stability, not attribution. Ridge lets
teammates and opponents leak into each other's estimates, both halves share
the schedule, so the leak is stable across halves and split-half reliability
counts it as signal (A9). The attribution factor removes that part. In each of
$N$ simulations, independent standard-normal offence and defence effects
$\beta^{(n)}$ are planted on the real design and fitted without noise at
$\lambda$, giving $\tilde\beta^{(n)}$. Then, per group,
\begin{equation}
  A_g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \corr_g\big(\tilde\beta^{(n)}, \beta^{(n)}\big)^2,
  \qquad
  A^{1/2}_g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
  \frac{\corr_g(\tilde\beta^{(n)}_{\mathcal A}, \beta^{(n)})\,\corr_g(\tilde\beta^{(n)}_{\mathcal B}, \beta^{(n)})}
       {\corr_g(\tilde\beta^{(n)}_{\mathcal A}, \tilde\beta^{(n)}_{\mathcal B})},
  \label{eq:attribution}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde\beta_{\mathcal A}$ and $\tilde\beta_{\mathcal B}$ are noise-free
fits on the two halves of a random game split. $\rho^{\mathrm{SH}}_g A_g$ is the
squared correlation of RAPM with the player's own effect, the reliability M4
needs; $A^{1/2}_g$ is its half-data counterpart. The two halves leak through
partly different teammates, which is why the half-data factor is a ratio and
not a square. Both assume effects independent of who plays with whom.

\paragraph{Per-season effects.}
M5 needs a season-level impact. Per-season effects are shrunk toward the
pooled ones: $o_{iq} = o_i + \Delta^{o}_{iq}$ and
$\delta_{iq} = \delta_i + \Delta^{\delta}_{iq}$, fitted with penalty
$\lambda_{\text{season}} \sum (\Delta^{o\,2}_{iq} + \Delta^{\delta\,2}_{iq})$ and
the home and intercept terms re-estimated. $\lambda_{\text{season}}$ is chosen
by 5-fold game-grouped cross-validation; at the chosen value,
\RapmLambdaSeason{}, the season deviations are shrunk almost to zero
(Section~\ref{sec:r-m3}), so the estimates are in effect pooled RAPM by season,
and we call them that.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{M4: the unmeasured share}
\label{sec:m4}

M4 is the paper's core measurement. Within a position group, it asks what
share of the variance of true on-pitch impact the individual statistics cannot
predict, after correcting for the noise in both.

\paragraph{Statistics and prediction.}
Under A11, a player's statistics are his per-90 non-penalty goals, penalty
goals, assists, yellow cards and red cards over the window, z-scored: a vector
$s_i$. Players with at least 900 minutes over the window enter. The
pre-registration's 900-minute floor reads as a minimum per player-season;
because M4 works on window totals, it is applied to window minutes, the same
floor that gives a player his own RAPM columns. Within group
$g$, a ridge
regression of RAPM on $s_i$, with unpenalised intercept and strength $\eta n$,
gives the prediction $f(s_i)$; $\eta$ is the value in
$\{10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, \dots, 10^{2}\}$ with the smallest out-of-fold squared
error over folds of players. The uncorrected fit is
\begin{equation}
  R^2_{\text{full}} = \max\big(\corr(\hat f^{\text{oof}}(s), \hat\beta), 0\big)^2,
  \qquad \sigma^{\text{naive}}_g = 1 - R^2_{\text{full}},
  \label{eq:r2full}
\end{equation}
with $\hat f^{\text{oof}}$ the out-of-fold prediction. It is the closest
quantity in this paper to the 22--38\% reported in the abstract of
\citet{gelade2020events}, which reports no correction for noise.

\paragraph{Cross-half estimator (A10).}
Suppose observed prediction and RAPM are true values plus independent errors.
Then their squared correlation is the true one times the product of their
reliabilities \citep{spearman1904proof}. The errors are not independent when
one match moves both a player's statistics and his stints' goals, and the
shared noise inflates the observed correlation and biases the unmeasured share
down. The cross-half estimator removes it. For each game split
$j = 1, \dots, J$ into halves $\mathcal A_j$ and $\mathcal B_j$, RAPM is fitted on
each half ($\hat\beta^{\mathcal A_j}$, $\hat\beta^{\mathcal B_j}$) and the
statistics are computed on the same halves ($s^{\mathcal A_j}$,
$s^{\mathcal B_j}$), so a prediction from one half never shares a match with the
RAPM of the other. With $P^{\mathcal A} = \hat f(s^{\mathcal A})$,
\begin{align}
  \bar r &= \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J} \tfrac12\Big[\corr\big(P^{\mathcal A_j}, \hat\beta^{\mathcal B_j}\big)
          + \corr\big(P^{\mathcal B_j}, \hat\beta^{\mathcal A_j}\big)\Big],
  \qquad R^2_{\text{obs}} = \max(\bar r, 0)^2,
  \label{eq:cross-r}\\
  \rho_P &= \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j} \corr\big(P^{\mathcal A_j}, P^{\mathcal B_j}\big),
  \qquad
  \rho_\beta = A^{1/2}_g\, \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j} \corr\big(\hat\beta^{\mathcal A_j}, \hat\beta^{\mathcal B_j}\big),
  \label{eq:cross-rel}\\
  R^2_{\text{true}} &= \frac{R^2_{\text{obs}}}{\rho_\beta\, \rho_P},
  \qquad
  \sigma_g = 1 - R^2_{\text{true}}, \qquad \sigma^{\text{clip}}_g = \min\big(\max(\sigma_g, 0), 1\big).
  \label{eq:share}
\end{align}
All reliabilities are at half length, matching the half-length correlation
$\bar r$. $R^2_{\text{true}}$ is clipped to $[0,1]$ (A7), because a
disattenuated value can leave it \citep{charles2005attenuation}; raw values
are reported. The component is RAPM total. The within-sample estimator, reported
as robustness, uses $R^2_{\text{obs}} = R^2_{\text{full}}$ and the stepped-up
full-length reliabilities with the full-data attribution factor $A_g$.

Two remarks bound the interpretation. First, $\sigma_g$ is the share of true
on-pitch impact that is not \emph{linearly} predictable from these five
statistics. In population, adding statistics cannot lower the $R^2$ of the best
linear predictor, so $\sigma_g$ is an upper bound on what any superset of them
would leave unexplained. Second, RAPM measures effects on goals while the
player is on the pitch; what he does off it is outside both sides.

\paragraph{The H3 contrast.}
H3 compares two pools of groups, each estimated as one group:
\begin{equation}
  D = \sigma^{\text{clip}}_{\text{CB+DM}} - \sigma^{\text{clip}}_{\text{CF+W}} .
  \label{eq:contrast}
\end{equation}
Three intervals are computed. (i) A player bootstrap resamples players within
each pool and re-estimates everything. (ii) The A7 joint bootstrap pairs each
player resample with RAPM replicate $b$ and the split of its own games, and
re-estimates both reliabilities. On synthetic leagues this second interval
adds a second draw of noise and overstates the spread (A9). (iii) The
parametric bootstrap of A9, which decides H3.

\paragraph{Parametric bootstrap on the real schedule (A9, A13).}
Effects are planted on the real stints and minutes with the generator of M9
(Section~\ref{sec:m9}), and M3 and M4 are re-run end to end on each of $S = 200$
simulated data sets with five game splits each. The generator's parameters are
calibrated to the real data (A13 (iv)). Planted offence and defence standard
deviations per group are set so that the simulated RAPM's split-half
covariance per group matches the real one, by multiplicative updates over a
few simulations. The measurable share per group is M4's cross-half estimate
(total, clipped). Intercept and home terms per competition-season are the real
fit's, with no match shock. The simulated box score's per-90 rate has the real
group mean of non-penalty goals plus assists, and a standard deviation chosen
so that its full-window reliability at the group's median minutes equals the
real reliability of M4's statistics prediction in that group. The planted
contrast of simulation $s$, $D_{0,s}$, is M4's own pooled estimand under the
generator, computed from that simulation's planted effects
(Section~\ref{sec:m9}; A18). With $\hat D$ the real estimate and
$\varepsilon_s = \hat D^\ast_s - D_{0,s}$ the simulated errors, the basic
(pivotal) interval is
\begin{equation}
  \big[\hat D - Q_{0.95}(\varepsilon),\; \hat D - Q_{0.05}(\varepsilon)\big].
  \label{eq:basic}
\end{equation}
M4 is biased on finite data (ridge leakage, the corrections, the clipping).
The basic interval treats the error as a pivot and so removes that bias,
whereas a percentile interval of the $\hat D^\ast_s$ would add it a second time.
Two coverages are reported: the share of simulations whose own player-bootstrap
interval covers its $D_{0,s}$, and the leave-one-out coverage of the parametric
interval.

\subsection{M5: specification uncertainty}
\label{sec:m5}

M5 refuses to pick one definition of ``best''. It ranks the pool under every
specification of a full factorial \citep{steegen2016multiverse,simonsohn2020specification}
and measures how much each choice moves each rank. Table~\ref{tab:dims} lists
the eight dimensions. Their product has
$4 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 5{,}760$ specifications.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{The dimensions of the multiverse (M5, as amended by A11 and A13).}
  \label{tab:dims}
  \small
  \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.6cm}Y@{}}
    \toprule
    Dimension & Levels \\
    \midrule
    Family & box: per-90 goals, non-penalty goals, assists;
             box+GD: box plus raw on-pitch goal difference per 90;
             impact: per-season RAPM offence and defence;
             hybrid: all six \\
    Weights & equal-z; first principal component; ridge regression on pooled RAPM; V1-like \\
    Position & none (z over the segment); within-group z; replacement level \\
    Context & none; Elo-partialled \\
    Aggregation & peak season; best three seasons; window mean; window volume; recency-weighted \\
    Competition & equal; Champions League and internationals $\times 1.5$; league only \\
    Minimum minutes & 900; 1,800 per season \\
    Shrinkage & raw; empirical Bayes \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\paragraph{One specification.}
Population statistics (means, standard deviations, quantiles, principal
components, ridge fits) are computed on every qualifying player-season,
pool or not; only the ranking is restricted to the pool. Outfield players and
goalkeepers are separate segments throughout. In order:
(1) the scope rows (league, ucl, intl) of a player-season are merged, rates
averaged with weights equal to the competition weight times minutes;
(2) a player-season qualifies if its minutes reach the threshold;
(3) empirical-Bayes shrinkage \citep{efron1975stein} replaces a rate $x$ with
$\bar x_g + \frac{m}{m+k}(x - \bar x_g)$, where $m$ is the season's minutes,
$\bar x_g$ the minutes-weighted mean of the player's group and $k$ the metric's
stabilisation constant from M1 (Equation~\eqref{eq:stab}) within that position
group (A16 (i), fixed before M5 ran: the outfield-pooled $k$ would count
differences between positions as signal). M1 has no goalkeeper constant for
goals, non-penalty goals and assists; post hoc, the run sets $k = 10^6$
minutes for them. Shrinkage then pulls every goalkeeper's rates almost to the
goalkeeper mean, and the z-score of step (5) rescales the small deviations
that remain, which are proportional to minutes times the raw deviation;

(4) Elo partialling subtracts, within each group, the metric's OLS slope on
team Elo times the centred Elo;
(5) position normalisation is a z-score over the segment, a z-score within the
group, or $(x - Q_{0.2,g}) / \mathrm{SD}$, the distance to the group's 20th
percentile on the segment's scale;
(6) the season score is $S_{iq} = \sum_k w_k \hat x_{iqk}$ with weights scaled
so $\sum_k |w_k| = 1$;
(7) seasons are aggregated (below);
(8) the pool is ranked, 1 = best, ties sharing the best rank, and a pool player
with no qualifying season ranks last. Shrinkage and Elo partialling are not
applied to RAPM, which is already shrunk and adjusted for teammates and
opponents.

\paragraph{Weights.}
Equal-z gives $1/p$ to each metric. The first principal component of the
normalised metrics is signed so that goals load positively. The ridge
regression of pooled RAPM total on the normalised metrics chooses its penalty
by 5-fold cross-validation with folds grouped by player; in the impact family
this would be circular, so it falls back to RAPM total, and in the hybrid
family the ridge uses the statistics only while the impact block carries half
the weight. The V1-like weights are A13's (Section~\ref{sec:m2}). Degenerate
duplicates (for example impact with and without shrinkage) are kept so that
the factorial stays balanced; the number of distinct rankings is reported.

\paragraph{Aggregation.}
With season scores $S_{iq}$ over qualifying seasons $q \in \mathcal Q_i$, minutes
$m_{iq}$, start year $t_q$, the last season $T$ in the data and the replacement
level $b$ (the 20th percentile of season scores over the segment, or within the
group under a position normalisation),
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{peak} &= \max_{q} S_{iq}, &
  \text{best three} &= \text{mean of the three largest } S_{iq},\\
  \text{mean} &= \frac{\sum_q m_{iq} S_{iq}}{\sum_q m_{iq}}, &
  \text{volume} &= \frac{1}{90}\sum_q m_{iq} \max(S_{iq} - b, 0),\\
  \text{recency} &= \frac{\sum_q \omega_{iq} S_{iq}}{\sum_q \omega_{iq}}, &
  \omega_{iq} &= m_{iq}\, 2^{-(T - t_q)/2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:aggregation}
\end{equation}
A player with fewer than three qualifying seasons takes the mean of those he
has under ``best three''.

\paragraph{Variance indices.}
Let $r_i(s)$ be player $i$'s rank under specification
$s = (s_1, \dots, s_8)$, with $s$ uniform on the factorial. For dimension $d$, the
first-order and total-order indices \citep{sobol2001sensitivity,homma1996importance,saltelli2010total}
are
\begin{equation}
  F_{id} = \frac{\Var\big(\E[r_i \mid s_d]\big)}{\Var(r_i)},
  \qquad
  T_{id} = 1 - \frac{\Var\big(\E[r_i \mid s_{-d}]\big)}{\Var(r_i)}
         = \frac{\E\big[\Var(r_i \mid s_{-d})\big]}{\Var(r_i)},
  \label{eq:sobol}
\end{equation}
where $s_{-d}$ is every dimension but $d$. On a complete, balanced factorial the
conditional expectations are cell means, so both indices are exact, with no
Monte Carlo estimator. $F_{id}$ is the share of rank variance that $d$ explains
alone; $T_{id}$ adds every interaction involving $d$. The indices are computed
on the original data. M5 also reports, per pool player, the share of
specifications that rank him first, in the top three and in the top ten, and
the specification curve.

\subsection{M6: recognition}
\label{sec:m6}

M6 asks what the Ballon d'Or vote rewards. Its universe and features were
fixed by A14 before any fit. Each edition $e \in \{2021, \dots, 2026\}$ has a
candidate universe $\mathcal U_e$: every outfield pool player with minutes in the
season the edition votes on, with zero points if unranked. Goalkeepers are
excluded, because the statistics block says nothing about them. The editions
of 2022 to 2026 vote on the seasons 2021/22 to 2025/26; the 2021 edition, the
last to vote on a calendar year, is mapped to 2020/21, an approximation. Each
candidate has four blocks of features, all for that season over every
competition:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{individual statistics}: goals, assists, minutes and non-penalty
    goals per 90 (A14's wording). We read it as season totals of goals and
    assists, minutes, and the per-90 rate of non-penalty goals; the other
    reading, per-90 goals, assists and non-penalty goals plus minutes, is fitted
    as a post-hoc robustness check;
  \item \emph{impact}: per-season RAPM offence and defence;
  \item \emph{team success}: indicators for a league title (first in the final
    table computed from the games, with 3, 1 and 0 points, then goal
    difference, then goals), a Champions League win, a lost Champions League
    final, and a senior international tournament win;
  \item \emph{reputation}: $\log(1 + \text{points in the previous edition})$,
    with 2019 for 2021 (A3), and the log market value at the last valuation on
    or before 30 June of the edition year.
\end{itemize}
A player counts for a club in a competition-season if he played at least one
minute for it there. No league title is awarded for the unfinished 2026 season
of calendar-year leagues. Finals are the games labelled ``Final'' in the data;
a drawn final is decided by the shoot-out where StatsBomb has the match. Every
national-team competition with a final in the data counts as a senior
tournament, and a final missing from the data gives no indicator.
Candidate-editions without a per-season RAPM estimate, or without a market
value on or before 30 June, are dropped and counted. Market values are crowd
estimates, not facts \citep{herm2014crowd}.

\paragraph{OLS.}
With $y_{ie} = \log(1 + \text{points}_{ie})$, edition fixed effects $\gamma_e$ and
the features $x_{ie}$,
\begin{equation}
  y_{ie} = \gamma_e + x_{ie}^\top \theta + \varepsilon_{ie}.
  \label{eq:ols}
\end{equation}
$R^2$ is the within-edition $R^2$: the fixed effects are always in the model and
are not a block, so the model with no block has $R^2 = 0$. Its
leave-one-edition-out version demeans $y$ and $x$ within each edition and scores
the held-out edition on its demeaned values.

\paragraph{Plackett--Luce.}
The ranked order is modelled with utilities $u_{ie} = x_{ie}^\top \beta$
\citep{plackett1975permutations,luce1959choice,hunter2004mm}. Unranked
candidates rank below every ranked player, with no order among themselves.
The likelihood is the top-$K$ Plackett--Luce likelihood
\begin{equation}
  L(\beta) = \prod_e \prod_{i \in \mathcal U_e\ \text{ranked}}
  \frac{\exp(u_{ie})}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal R_{ie}} \exp(u_{je})},
  \label{eq:pl}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal R_{ie}$ holds every candidate of edition $e$ not ranked strictly
above $i$, unranked candidates included. It equals the full Plackett--Luce
probability summed over every order of the unranked. Tied players share one
risk set. Utilities are defined only up to a constant within an edition, so
there is no intercept. Fit quality is McFadden's pseudo-$R^2$,
$1 - \log L(\hat\beta) / \log L(0)$, with $L(0)$ the uniform model.

\paragraph{Shapley decomposition.}
For a model's fit $v(\mathcal C)$ (within-edition $R^2$, or pseudo-$R^2$) on a
coalition $\mathcal C$ of the block set $\mathcal K$ of size $|\mathcal K| = 4$,
block $k$'s share is its exact Shapley value \citep{shapley1953value}
\begin{equation}
  \varphi_k = \sum_{\mathcal C \subseteq \mathcal K \setminus \{k\}}
  \frac{|\mathcal C|!\,(|\mathcal K| - |\mathcal C| - 1)!}{|\mathcal K|!}
  \big[v(\mathcal C \cup \{k\}) - v(\mathcal C)\big],
  \label{eq:shapley}
\end{equation}
computed over all sixteen coalitions. For OLS this is the LMG decomposition
\citep{lindeman1980multivariate,gromping2007relimp}. The shares sum to the full
model's fit. H6 compares $\varphi_{\text{team}}$ with $\varphi_{\text{stats}}$
in the OLS model (A1). Intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples players,
each a cluster of all his editions: six editions are too few clusters, and a
player's residual persists from one edition to the next.

\paragraph{Residuals.}
A positive residual means the vote rewarded the player beyond what all four
blocks predict. In OLS it is $y_{ie} - \hat y_{ie}$. In Plackett--Luce it is
expected position minus observed position, with
$\E[\text{pos}_{ie}] = 1 + \sum_{j \ne i} \big(1 + e^{u_{ie} - u_{je}}\big)^{-1}$,
the pairwise marginal of a Plackett--Luce ranking, which has the
Bradley--Terry form \citep{bradley1952paired}.

\subsection{M7: on-ball value and big games}
\label{sec:m7}

\paragraph{M7a: visibility of on-ball value (exploratory).}
On the StatsBomb sample, M7a asks how well box-score per-90 rates predict an
event metric's per-90 rate (xT added, progressive passes and carries, passes
into the box, pressures, and others). The unit is a player-competition. A ridge
regression of the target rate $Y$ on the box rates gives an out-of-fold
prediction $P$ (folds by player). Off-ball value is in no event data; this
measures only what events can see. In each of several random match splits,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{PY} = \tfrac12\big[\corr(P_{\mathcal A}, Y_{\mathcal B}) + \corr(P_{\mathcal B}, Y_{\mathcal A})\big],
  \qquad
  r^2_{\text{cross}} = \operatorname{sign}(c)\, c^2,
  \quad c = \frac{\overline{\rho_{PY}}}{\sqrt{\overline{r_{PP}}\;\overline{r_{YY}}}},
  \label{eq:visibility}
\end{equation}
with $r_{PP}$ and $r_{YY}$ the half-length split-half correlations and bars
denoting means over splits. The within-sample version
$r^2_{\text{disatt}} = R^2_{\text{cv}} / (\rho_Y \rho_P)$, with stepped-up
reliabilities, is shown beside it. Because one match can move both box score
and target, it is biased upward and can exceed 1. This contrast motivated A10.
$r^2_{\text{cross}}$ is a lower bound on what the best linear box-score
summary could see, because the weights are fitted on noisy rates.

\paragraph{M7b: is ``big-game player'' a stable trait? (H7).}
For player $i$ in period $p \in \{1, 2\}$ (2020-01-01 to 2022-12-31 and
2023-01-01 to 2026-08-31), let $x^{\mathrm{KO}}_{ip}$ and $x^{\mathrm{N}}_{ip}$ be
his non-penalty goals plus assists per 90 (A4) in knockout and non-knockout
matches of every competition, $d^{\mathrm{KO}}_{ip}$ and $d^{\mathrm{N}}_{ip}$
the minutes, and
$\bar x_{ip}$ his pooled rate. His over-performance is the difference divided by
its Poisson standard error under the null of one common rate:
\begin{equation}
  Z_{ip} = \frac{x^{\mathrm{KO}}_{ip} - x^{\mathrm N}_{ip}}
  {\sqrt{90\, \bar x_{ip}\big(1/d^{\mathrm{KO}}_{ip} + 1/d^{\mathrm N}_{ip}\big)}} .
  \label{eq:overperf}
\end{equation}
A match is a knockout if its round is neither a league matchday nor a group
stage (the league phase of UEFA competitions from 2024 counts as a group), so
domestic cups, super cups and qualifying rounds count as knockouts alongside
the later rounds of continental and international tournaments. Only outfield
players are analysed. A player enters if, in both periods, he has at least 360
knockout minutes, some non-knockout minutes and a non-zero pooled rate (A4).
Persistence is
$r = \corr(Z_{i1}, Z_{i2})$ across entering players, with a percentile
bootstrap interval over players and a one-sided permutation p-value
$(1 + \#\{r_{\text{perm}} \ge r\}) / (n_{\text{perm}} + 1)$, since a trait can only
appear as a positive correlation. A placebo replaces the real knockout games
with a random set of non-knockout games of the same share; a sound test gives
a correlation near zero there.

\paragraph{The A5 power rule.}
A low $r$ means little if $Z$ is mostly noise, because unreliability
attenuates any true correlation \citep{spearman1904proof}. Let $R_1$ and $R_2$
be the within-period split-half reliabilities of $Z$: in each of 200 random
splits, a player's knockout and non-knockout matches of the period are dealt
alternately into two halves, $Z$ is recomputed on each half, and the
correlation of the halves across players is stepped up with
Equation~\eqref{eq:sb}. A true correlation of 0.3 would be observed, in
expectation, as
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\text{obs}} = 0.3\,\sqrt{R_1 R_2},
  \qquad
  \text{power} \approx \Phi\big(\sqrt{n - 3}\,\operatorname{artanh}(\rho_{\text{obs}}) - z_{1-\alpha}\big),
  \label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
with $n$ the number of entering players, $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution
function, $z_{1-\alpha}$ its $1-\alpha$ quantile and $\operatorname{artanh}$ the
Fisher transformation. A5 left the test level and the power method open. The
analysis used a one-sided Pearson test at $\alpha$ = \HSevenAlpha{} and
computed power by simulation: 20,000 samples of $n$ players whose observed
scores have the estimated reliabilities and whose true scores correlate at 0.3,
counting the samples in which the test rejects. The Fisher approximation in
Equation~\eqref{eq:power} gives nearly the same value. Both choices were
recorded after H7 had been computed (A16 (iii)) and are not pre-registered.
H7 is concluded (``not a stable trait'') only if $r < 0.2$ and the power is at
least 0.8; otherwise it is reported as inconclusive (A5).

\paragraph{H8.}
H8 is not tested (A6, A11, A15). Its test compares a coarse, zone-based xG
available for many leagues with StatsBomb xG, and the only candidate source of
coarse shot data was withdrawn before any analysis used it.

\subsection{M8: synthesis}
\label{sec:m8}

M8 mixes the three sources of uncertainty into one distribution of ranks. A
draw is indexed by $(s, b, u)$: a specification $s$ of the set $\mathcal S$ of
M5's 5,760 specifications, a
sampling replicate $b \in \{1, \dots, 200\}$ and a draw $u \in \{1, \dots, U\}$ of the
unmeasured component, with $U = 4$ (A13 (ii)). Specifications and replicates
are finite populations, weighted equally. In replicate $b$, one resample of
games drives pooled RAPM, per-season RAPM and the player-season statistics,
and M5 is rerun on it (A13 (iii)). Let $x_{sb,i}$ be player $i$'s M5 score.

\paragraph{The mixture.}
The draw's score is
\begin{equation}
  y_{sbu,i} = x_{sb,i} + \tau_{sb,i}\, \zeta_{sbu,i},
  \qquad \zeta_{sbu,i} \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\ \text{independent},
  \label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
and players are ranked within their segment as in M5:
$r_i = 1 + \#\{j : y_j > y_i\}$, a player without a qualifying season last.

\paragraph{Scale of the unmeasured component.}
In group $g$, write true impact $T = V + W$, with $V$ the part the statistics can
see and $W$ uncorrelated with it. M4's share is
$\sigma_g = 1 - R^2_{\text{true}} = \Var(W)/\Var(T)$, so
$\Var(W) = \frac{\sigma_g}{1 - \sigma_g}\Var(V)$. Taking a box-score score as $V$
up to scale gives the variance of the part it cannot see. A specification that
is only partly box score adds unseen variance only for that part. Let
$\omega_s$ be the share of specification $s$'s absolute weights on the box
metrics (goals, non-penalty goals, assists), taken from M5's weights on the
original data. On-pitch goal difference counts with RAPM: as an on-pitch measure
it already contains the unseen part. So $\omega_s = 1$ for the box family and
$\omega_s = 0$ for impact, where RAPM's noise is already in $b$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \tau^2_{sb,i} = \frac{\omega_s \tilde\sigma_{g(i)}}{1 - \omega_s \tilde\sigma_{g(i)}}\;
  \widehat{\Var}_{g(i)}(x_{sb}),
  \qquad
  \tilde\sigma_g = \min\big(\sigma^{\text{clip}}_g, 0.95\big),
  \label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
with the robust variance
$\widehat{\Var}_g(x_{sb}) = \big(1.4826 \MAD_g(x_{sb})\big)^2$,
where $\sigma^{\text{clip}}_g$ is M4's clipped cross-half share for RAPM total
(A13 (ii)) and $\MAD_g$ is taken over the finite scores of the pool players of
group $g$ in the segment. The cap applies here only, because the ratio diverges
at 1. The robust variance equals the variance under normality; the sample
variance would let a leader's lead inflate $\tau$, so that no lead could ever be
decided. If the pool is selected on the visible score, its spread understates
the population's and $\tau$ is too small, which is conservative for H1: it
understates rank uncertainty. M4's share is held at its point estimate; its
own uncertainty is not propagated. Nor is the uncertainty in RAPM's
attribution of goals among teammates: that leakage is stable across game
resamples, so the replicates $b$ do not contain it, and the impact family has
$\tau = 0$. Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (f) adds it post hoc.

\paragraph{Outputs.}
Per player: $P(\#1)$, $P(\text{top-3})$, $P(\text{top-10})$, the median rank and a
90\% rank interval, from the mixture's rank distribution. Pairwise, the Gaussian
draw is integrated exactly:
\begin{equation}
  P(i \text{ above } j) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal S| B}\sum_{s, b}
  \Phi\!\left(\frac{x_{sb,i} - x_{sb,j}}{\sqrt{\tau^2_{sb,i} + \tau^2_{sb,j}}}\right),
  \label{eq:pairwise}
\end{equation}
with exact ties in $x$ counted one half, so the matrix is complementary. A pair is
decided when $P(i \text{ above } j) \ge 0.9$ or $\le 0.1$; H1's statistic is the
share of decided outfield pairs.

\paragraph{Nested decomposition.}
Each player's rank variance splits exactly into a specification, a sampling and
an unmeasured term:
\begin{equation}
  \Var(r_i) = \underbrace{\Var_s\big(\E[r_i \mid s]\big)}_{\text{specification}}
  + \underbrace{\E_s\big[\Var_b\big(\E[r_i \mid s, b]\big)\big]}_{\text{sampling}}
  + \underbrace{\E_{s,b}\big[\Var_u(r_i \mid s, b)\big]}_{\text{unmeasured}},
  \label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
with population variances over the equally weighted $s$ and $b$. The inner mean
is exact, $\E_u[r_i \mid s, b] = 1 + \sum_{j \ne i} \Phi\big((x_{sb,j} - x_{sb,i}) /
\sqrt{\tau^2_{sb,i} + \tau^2_{sb,j}}\big)$; $\E_u[r_i^2 \mid s, b]$ is the mean over
the $U$ draws, and the total is assembled from the same ingredients, so the
three terms sum to it exactly and the specification and sampling terms carry no
Monte Carlo noise. H4's statistic is the mean, over the players ranked in the
top ten by the median specification, of specification over sampling variance.

\paragraph{Median specification.}
The median specification is the specification whose outfield ranking on the
original data is nearest, in Spearman's footrule (the $L_1$ distance between
rank vectors), to the coordinatewise median of the outfield rankings across
specifications, lowest identifier on ties. The coordinatewise median is the
$L_1$ centre of the rankings, so this is the actual specification closest to the
centre of the multiverse. It defines H4's top ten and the fixed estimator of the
rank confidence sets.

\paragraph{Rank confidence sets.}
At the median specification, marginal and simultaneous 90\% confidence sets for
ranks follow \citet{mogstad2024ranks}, single-step and two-sided. With scores
$x_j$ on the original data, $D_{jk} = x_j - x_k$, its bootstrap standard deviation
$\mathrm{se}_{jk}$ over the $B$ replicates and
$t^\ast_{jk} = |D^\ast_{jk} - D_{jk}| / \mathrm{se}_{jk}$, the critical value $c$ is the
0.9 quantile over replicates of $\max_k t^\ast_{jk}$ (marginal set of player $j$)
or of $\max_{j,k} t^\ast_{jk}$ (simultaneous sets). Player $k$ is above $j$ when
$D_{jk} + c\,\mathrm{se}_{jk} < 0$ and below when $D_{jk} - c\,\mathrm{se}_{jk} > 0$,
and the set is
\begin{equation}
  \big[\,1 + \#\{k \text{ above } j\},\; n - \#\{k \text{ below } j\}\,\big].
  \label{eq:mogstad}
\end{equation}
These sets account for sampling only, at one definition. They complement the
percentile intervals of bootstrap ranks, which can be inconsistent near ties
\citep{hall2009bootstrap}.

\paragraph{Agreement of views.}
Three views rank the pool: statistics (a player's mean rank over the box-family
specifications), impact (over the impact-family specifications), and
recognition. The recognition view is the sum, over the editions of 2021 to
2026, of the player's share of that edition's total points, zero for unranked
pool players (A19). A13 (v) had fixed the raw sum of points; the ballot changed
in 2022 and in 2024, so an edition's total points vary several-fold and the raw
sum weights the last three editions most. The correction was made after M8
had run, and the raw-sum value is reported beside the corrected one. The box+GD and hybrid families mix the two kinds of measure
and enter neither view. Agreement is Kendall's $W$ with the tie correction
\citep{kendall1939rankings}: with $m = 3$ views, $n$ players, $R_i$ the sum of
player $i$'s mid-ranks over views and $T_v = \sum (t^3 - t)$ over the tie groups of
view $v$,
\begin{equation}
  W = \frac{12 \sum_i (R_i - \bar R)^2}{m^2 (n^3 - n) - m \sum_v T_v},
  \qquad
  \bar\rho_{\text{Spearman}} = \frac{mW - 1}{m - 1}.
  \label{eq:kendall}
\end{equation}
Under independent random views $\E[W] = 1/m$, not 0, while the mean pairwise
Spearman correlation has expectation 0.

\subsection{M9: recovery study}
\label{sec:m9}

M9 is exploratory. It plants player qualities on the real schedule, draws
goals and box scores, runs the estimators, and asks how often they recover the
planted best player. It measures how often two of our estimators, pooled
RAPM and a box composite, recover the planted best player from data of this
size; it is not a bound on what any method could decide. The same generator
drives the parametric bootstrap of H3.

\paragraph{Generator.}
The schedule is given, not generated: the real stints, who was on the pitch in
each, and therefore every player's real minutes. Player $i$ in group $g$ gets
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  o_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \varsigma^2_{o,g}), \qquad
  \delta_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \varsigma^2_{\delta,g}), \qquad
  \beta_i = o_i + \delta_i, \\
  z_i = \sqrt{\pi_g}\,\frac{\beta_i}{\varsigma_{\beta,g}} + \sqrt{1 - \pi_g}\, \epsilon_i,
  \qquad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:gen-effects}
\end{equation}
independently, where $\varsigma_{\beta,g}$ is the planted standard deviation of
$\beta$ and $\pi_g$ the measurable share: in the population of a group, $z$
explains a share $\pi_g$ of the variance of $\beta$. The box score is a count
drawn per player-match from a Poisson law with per-90 rate
$\max(\epsilon_0, \mu_g + s_g z_i)$, scaled by minutes over 90. A side's goals in
stint $\ell$ are Poisson with per-90 rate
\begin{equation}
  \max\Big(\epsilon_0,\; \mu_{\kappa} + \tfrac12 h_\kappa \pm \tfrac12 h_\kappa
  + \sum_{i \in \mathcal L_{\ell a}} o_i - \sum_{j \in \mathcal L_{\ell\bar a}} \delta_j\Big),
  \label{eq:gen-goals}
\end{equation}
with $+$ for the home side and $-$ for the away side, scaled by $d_\ell / 90$;
$\epsilon_0 > 0$ is a small floor. Box score and stint goals are independent given
the effects. For H3, the planted contrast of simulation $s$ is
$D_{0,s} = (1 - R^2_{\mathcal H}) - (1 - R^2_{\mathcal W})$, where $R^2_{\mathcal P}$
is the $R^2$ of an OLS, with intercept, of the planted total effect $\beta_i$
on the player's expected per-90 statistics, over the players entering M4 in
pool $\mathcal P$: CB+DM ($\mathcal H$) or CF+W ($\mathcal W$). This is M4's own
pooled estimand under the generator (A18, a correction made after the result).
Before A18 the planted value was the variance-weighted average of the groups'
shares, which ignores that M4 fits one model across a pool's groups.

\paragraph{Recovery.}
Each simulation plants fresh effects for everyone, pool included, so the gap
between the planted first and second of the pool,
$\Delta_{12} = \beta_{(1)} - \beta_{(2)}$, varies between simulations. For each
simulation we record whether pooled RAPM total, and a box composite (the mean of
the per-90 box metrics, each z-scored within group: M5's equal-z, within-group
specification), put the planted first of the pool first, and the mean absolute
rank error $\frac1n \sum_i |\hat r_i - r_i|$ within the pool. Results are averaged
in bins of $\Delta_{12}$ with equal numbers of simulations.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports the pre-registered analysis, which decides the
hypotheses. It does not adjust for league strength; Section~\ref{sec:league}
shows why that is a flaw and what an adjustment changes.

The data do not name a best player. Under the mixture of definitions, samples
and unmeasured draws (M8), the outfield player most likely to rank first is
\TopPlayerName{}, with a probability of \TopPlayerPFirst{}, and only
\SharePairsDecided{} of the outfield pool's pairwise orderings are decided. What moves a
player's rank most is the definition of ``best'', and within the definition it
is the kind of evidence a score is built from (box scores, on-pitch goal
difference or RAPM), not the way seasons are combined. On-pitch impact is far
less visible to the box score for centre-backs and defensive midfielders than
for attackers, although the correction behind that estimate over-corrects for
attackers and is sensitive for defenders. The leader's lead rests on RAPM,
whose division of goals among teammates the primary synthesis does not treat
as uncertain. In the point estimates the Ballon d'Or vote follows individual
statistics more than team success, but that order is fragile.
The subsections below follow the modules. Sections~\ref{sec:league}
and~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} hold the analyses added after the results were seen,
each with its motivation.

\subsection{M1: sampling noise}
\label{sec:r-m1}

For player-seasons of at least 900 minutes, the split-half reliability of
non-penalty goals plus assists per 90 is \RelNpga{}, with \NReliabilityUnits{}
player-seasons per split on average. For goals it is \RelGoals{}, for
non-penalty goals \RelNpGoals{}, for assists \RelAssists{} and for raw on-pitch
goal difference \RelOnPitchGd{}. The stabilisation constant of non-penalty goals
plus assists is $k$ = \StabKNpga{} minutes, so its reliability reaches 0.7 at
\StabSevenNpga{} minutes; for on-pitch goal difference, $k$ = \StabKOnPitchGd{}
minutes (Table~\ref{tab:reliability}, Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}).

Scoring rates stabilise fastest. Assists stabilise more slowly than goals,
yellow cards more slowly still, and red cards carry almost no stable signal at
season level. A regular starter's season is therefore enough to rank scoring
rates roughly, but not to separate two players whose true rates are close.
These reliabilities pool every outfield position, and part of the stable
variance they measure is the difference between positions. Within a position
group, where true scoring rates differ less, goals, non-penalty goals and
assists are much less reliable; on-pitch goal difference and cards are not.
This is why M5 shrinks toward the group mean with within-group constants
(A16 (i)). The
StatsBomb rows of Table~\ref{tab:reliability} are exploratory: few
player-seasons reach the floor on StatsBomb matches, almost all of them from
four club teams, so they are not comparable with the Transfermarkt rows.

\subsection{M2: structure and double counting}
\label{sec:r-m2}

This subsection is exploratory. The number of components that parallel
analysis retains among the \NMetricsStructure{} non-redundant metrics is
\NComponentsRetained{}; the participation ratio is \ParticipationRatio{} and the
entropy effective number \EntropyEffective{}, against a maximum of
\NMetricsStructure{} (Figure~\ref{fig:scree}). The metrics are therefore close
to independent: a box score of this kind measures several different things
weakly, not one thing many times. The strong dependence is the one built into
the definitions. Goals and non-penalty goals have a Spearman correlation of
\CorrGoalsNpGoals{}, because most goals are not penalties, and any score that
weights both counts scoring twice (Figure~\ref{fig:corr-heatmap}).

The V1-like composite does exactly that. Its variance is \VOneInflation{} times
what its weights imply under independence, and the first principal component
carries \VOnePcOneShare{} of it: the composite is, in effect, one scoring
dimension. Goals and non-penalty goals together claim \VOneGoalsClaimed{} of its
variance and carry \VOneGoalsEffective{} (Table~\ref{tab:composite}). The
double counting that V1's author conceded is real in kind; in our imitation it
shifts the shares modestly and concentrates the composite on scoring.

The team ICC is \IccNpGoals{} for non-penalty goals, \IccAssists{} for assists
and \IccOnPitchGd{} for on-pitch goal difference (Table~\ref{tab:icc}). Raw
on-pitch goal difference, the plus-minus that public sites show, is mostly a
property of the team; this is why M3 adjusts it for teammates and opponents.
The near-zero values for scoring rates do not mean that a striker's output is
independent of his team. Within a club, a centre-back and a striker differ far
more than clubs do, so ICC(1) over all outfield positions is small for any
metric that varies mostly by position. The penalty-goal ICC is negative, which
is consistent with each club having one designated taker. The intervals are
approximate (A17): the club bootstrap is offset from the estimate on the real
data, by more than on the synthetic panels that motivated the standard
interval.

\subsection{M3: on-pitch impact}
\label{sec:r-m3}

The primary fit has \NStints{} stints, \NCompetitionSeasons{}
competition-seasons and \NPlayersOwnColumns{} players with their own columns;
cross-validation chooses $\lambda$ = \RapmLambda{}. The split-half reliability
of RAPM is \RelRapmOffence{} for offence, \RelRapmDefence{} for defence and
\RelRapmTotal{} for the total. The attribution factor of the total is
\AttrFullTotal{} on full data and \AttrHalfTotal{} on half data, so the
attributed reliability of RAPM total is \RelRapmTotalAttributed{}
(Table~\ref{tab:rapm-reliability}).

RAPM is stable but poorly attributed. In every group, less than half of its
stable variance belongs to the player's own effect when effects are planted
independently of who plays with whom; the rest is teammates and opponents
leaking through the ridge. Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (a) shows that this
factor depends strongly on how quality is spread across teammates.

\NPoolRapmExcludesZero{} of the \NPoolOutfield{} outfield pool players have a
90\% interval of RAPM total that excludes zero (Figure~\ref{fig:rapm-pool}).
That says they are good, not that they can be told apart: the pool was
selected for quality, and the intervals of the leading players overlap.
\TopPlayerName{} has the highest pooled RAPM total in the outfield pool,
\PoolTopRapm{} goals per 90 (bootstrap SE \PoolTopRapmSe{}). In domestic
leagues the home term is \HomeTermEmpty{} goals per 90 in 2019/20 and 2020/21
and \HomeTermOther{} in the later seasons. This drop is our own estimate; the
literature we cite documents a fall in home advantage in German top-division
ghost games of 2019/20 \citep{fischer2021ghost} and a narrower home--away gap in
cards \citep{bryson2021crowd}, not a size in goals for these seasons. Adding Elo
as a covariate barely reorders players: the rank correlation between the two
fits is \RapmEloSpearman{}.

Per-season RAPM is shrunk toward the pooled fit with the penalty that
cross-validation chooses, $\lambda_{\text{season}}$ = \RapmLambdaSeason{}. At
that penalty a player's season estimates barely move: across players with
their own columns, the within-player standard deviation of per-season RAPM
total is \RapmSeasonWithinSd{} goals per 90, against a between-player standard
deviation of \RapmSeasonBetweenSd{}. The per-season estimates are therefore
pooled RAPM by season, close to a career constant. Two consequences follow.
In M5, an impact-family score hardly changes from season to season, so the
choice of time aggregation can do little to the rank of a player who leads on
RAPM. In M6, the impact block is close to time-invariant and borrows
information from seasons after the vote, so its small Shapley share is partly
an artefact of this look-ahead and of the lack of season-to-season variation.

\subsection{M4: the unmeasured share}
\label{sec:r-m4}

Without correction, the statistics explain between \RTwoFullMin{} and
\RTwoFullMax{} of the variance of RAPM total across outfield groups, least for
centre-backs and most for centre-forwards. The closest published figure is
the 22--38\% reported in the abstract of \citet{gelade2020events}, for richer
event statistics and with no correction for noise; our raw values are at or
below it.
Both sides are noisy at half length: the half-length reliability of RAPM is low
in every group, and that of the statistics' prediction is lowest for
goalkeepers and centre-backs (Table~\ref{tab:unmeasured}). The correction
divides by the product of the two, so it is large and sensitive.

With the cross-half correction, the unmeasured share is \ShareHigh{} for
centre-backs and defensive midfielders pooled and \ShareLow{} for
centre-forwards and wingers pooled (Figure~\ref{fig:unmeasured-share}). By
group, it is largest for goalkeepers, whose impact statistics about scoring
cannot see, although its interval runs from \ShareGKLo{} to \ShareGKHi{}
around \ShareGK{}; large for centre-backs and defensive midfielders;
intermediate for full-backs and central midfielders; and at or near zero for
wingers, attacking midfielders and centre-forwards. The pooled CB+DM share is
larger than the shares of both its groups. That is not an error: M4 fits one
model across a pool's groups, whose box-score means differ for reasons
unrelated to impact, so a pool's estimand is not an average of its groups'
(A18).

For centre-forwards and attacking midfielders the corrected $R^2_{\text{true}}$
exceeds 1, and \NGroupsClipped{} groups are clipped. A true $R^2$ cannot exceed
1, so the correction over-corrects there: the observed cross-half correlation
is larger than the two reliabilities allow. The likeliest cause is the
attribution factor, which assumes that player effects are independent of who
plays with whom. If good attackers play beside good teammates, more of RAPM's
stable variance is the player's own than the factor credits, its reliability
is understated, and the corrected $R^2$ is inflated.
Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (a) supports this reading. The attackers' share
is zero only because it is clipped, and it should be read as ``not
resolved''. The defenders' share is just as sensitive to the attribution
factor: with the bias-corrected shares of Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (b), every
outfield group except centre-forwards has a share of at least
\BiasShareOtherLo{}, centre-backs and defensive midfielders included. The size
of the H3 contrast is therefore uncertain beyond its interval; its sign is
what the analysis supports.

The H3 contrast is \ShareContrast{} (raw: \ShareContrastRaw{}). Its 90\%
parametric basic interval, which decides H3, is
[\ShareContrastParamLo, \ShareContrastParamHi]; for the raw contrast it is
[\ShareContrastParamRawLo, \ShareContrastParamRawHi]. The player bootstrap gives
[\ShareContrastPlayerLo, \ShareContrastPlayerHi] and the A7 joint bootstrap
[\ShareContrastJointLo, \ShareContrastJointHi] (Table~\ref{tab:h3},
Figure~\ref{fig:h3-parametric}). H3: \VerdictHThree{}.

The planted value of each simulation is M4's own pooled estimand under the
generator, a definition corrected after the result (A18). It averages
\HThreePlanted{} over the simulations; the earlier definition, the
variance-weighted average of the groups' shares, gave \HThreePlantedWeighted{}.
Against the corrected value the estimator is biased upward by
\HThreeMeanError{} on average, a bias the basic interval removes. The
leave-one-out coverage of the parametric interval is \ParamCoverageLoo{}, while
the player-bootstrap interval covers the planted contrast in
\ParamCoverageMFour{} of simulations.

The generator fits the real data imperfectly. On the real data the CF+W pool
is clipped; in the simulations either pool is clipped in \HThreeSimClipped{} of
runs, and the simulated raw contrast reaches the real one in only
\HThreeSimReachReal{} of them. The real estimate sits in the far tail of what
this generator produces. The parametric interval treats the estimator's error
as a pivot, and that assumption is least safe exactly there. The assortative
planting of Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (a), which keeps the real teammate
structure, does reach the real level.

The rows of Table~\ref{tab:h3} are not on the same footing. The A7 joint
bootstrap uses only the players present in every RAPM replicate's split
(\NHThreeHighJoint{} in CB+DM, against \NHThreeHigh{} for the other rows), and it
is wider, as the synthetic leagues predicted (A9). The within-sample estimator,
which A10 demoted because shared match noise biases it, uses full-length
reliabilities; it gives a clipped contrast of \ShareContrastWithin{}.

\subsection{M5: specification uncertainty}
\label{sec:r-m5}

The 5,760 specifications produce \NDistinctRankingsOutfield{} distinct outfield
rankings and \NDistinctRankingsGoalkeeper{} distinct goalkeeper rankings: most
definitions order the pool differently. \NPlayersEverFirst{} outfield pool
players are ranked first by at least one specification
(Table~\ref{tab:multiverse}). \MultiverseTopName{} is ranked first most often,
by \MultiverseTopPFirst{} of specifications. His firsts come almost all from
the two families that contain RAPM: \TopFirstImpact{} of the impact-family and
\TopFirstHybrid{} of the hybrid specifications rank him first, against
\TopFirstBox{} of the box-family ones and almost none of the box+GD ones
(Table~\ref{tab:firsts}, a post-hoc breakdown). The second most frequent
leader, Julián Quiñones, is ranked first by \QuinonesSpecShare{} of
specifications on the strength of his two qualifying seasons, both in the
Saudi league (2024/25 and 2025/26); his firsts come mostly from the mean and
recency aggregations (Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (c)).

For the rank of the player with the highest M8 $P(\#1)$, the dimension with the
largest total-order index is \HFiveMaxDim{}, at \HFiveMaxTotal{}; the index of
time aggregation is \HFiveAggTotal{} (Figure~\ref{fig:total-order}). H5:
\VerdictHFive{}. For this leader the outcome was close to automatic. He leads on
RAPM, and pooled RAPM by season barely varies within a player
(Section~\ref{sec:r-m3}), so time aggregation can hardly move his
impact-family rank: \TopPlayerName{} is ranked first by every impact-family
specification that does not aggregate by volume. The impact family is also
the least varied: it yields \NDistinctRankingsImpact{} distinct outfield
rankings among its 1,440 specifications, while each of the other families
yields a different ranking for almost every specification. A post-hoc reading
of the result is that what ``best'' means is decisive, but that the decisive
part is what a score is built from, not over which seasons it is taken. The
family has the largest total-order index for most of the players ever ranked
first. For each of them, the first-order indices of the eight
dimensions sum to most of the rank variance, so the choices matter more on
their own than through their interactions.

\subsection{M6: recognition}
\label{sec:r-m6}

The recognition universe has \NBdoRows{} candidate-editions and
\NBdoCandidates{} distinct players; \NBdoDropped{} candidate-editions were dropped
for lack of a per-season RAPM estimate and \NBdoDroppedValuation{} for lack of a
market value (A14). The OLS model has a within-edition $R^2$ of \OlsRTwo{}
(leave one edition out: \OlsRTwoLoeo{}), and the Plackett--Luce model a
pseudo-$R^2$ of \PlPseudoRTwo{}. In OLS, the Shapley shares are
\ShapleyStatsOls{} for individual statistics, \ShapleyImpactOls{} for impact,
\ShapleyTeamOls{} for team success and \ShapleyRepOls{} for reputation. The
90\% intervals are [\ShapleyTeamOlsLo, \ShapleyTeamOlsHi] for team success and
[\ShapleyStatsOlsLo, \ShapleyStatsOlsHi] for statistics, and team success
exceeds statistics in \ShapleyTeamAboveStatsBoot{} of bootstrap replicates. The
Plackett--Luce shares are \ShapleyStatsPl{}, \ShapleyImpactPl{},
\ShapleyTeamPl{} and \ShapleyRepPl{} in the same order; the two models
\OlsPlAgreement{} on the order of team success and statistics
(Table~\ref{tab:recognition}, Figure~\ref{fig:shapley}). H6: \VerdictHSix{}.

In the point estimates the vote follows individual statistics more than team
success, and on-pitch impact as RAPM measures it earns almost nothing. The
order of the first two is fragile. Team success exceeds statistics in
\ShapleyTeamAboveStatsBoot{} of bootstrap replicates, and in
\CuratedTeamAboveStatsBoot{} once team honours are curated
(Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (e)). Under the other reading of A14's statistics
block (per-90 rates plus minutes; post hoc), the statistics share is
\PerNinetyStatsOls{} and the team share \PerNinetyTeamOls{}; with curated
honours, the same reading puts team success first (\CuratedPerNinetyTeamOls{}
against \CuratedPerNinetyStatsOls{}). H6 is refuted as pre-registered, but the
evidence against it is weak.

Three features of the blocks bear on that verdict. First, the team block is
measured with gaps that bias it down, toward refuting H6. League titles come
from tables computed from the games, which miss titles decided by play-offs.
Whole tournaments are absent from the data (the 2021 Copa Am\'erica and the
2021 and 2023 Africa Cups of Nations), as are the Gold Cups, the Nations League
finals and the 2025/26 Champions League final, and the 2026 World Cup stops at
its group stage. The largest positive OLS residuals, players the vote rewarded
beyond all four blocks, belong to 2026 candidates, whose vote covered that
World Cup. Second, the statistics block absorbs part of team success: season
totals of goals and assists, and minutes, grow with a deep run in a cup or a
tournament, so a winner's statistics carry some of his team's success. The
missing tournaments remove those matches from the statistics block too.
Third, the impact block is pooled RAPM by season, close to time-invariant and
informed by later seasons (Section~\ref{sec:r-m3}), so its small share says
little about whether voters reward on-pitch impact.

\section{League-strength-adjusted analysis (post hoc)}
\label{sec:league}

The pre-registered analysis has a specification flaw, found after its results:
nothing in it adjusts for the strength of the league a player plays in. This
section shows the flaw, describes an adjustment, and reports what it changes.
The method was written down before any league-adjusted estimate existed (A20)
and amended twice before any league-adjusted ranking existed (A21, A22); a
validation and a contamination term were added after the ranking had run
(A23). All of it came after the results of Section~\ref{sec:results} were
known. Everything in this section is therefore post hoc and exploratory. The
hypotheses stay decided by the pre-registered analysis.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The flaw}
\label{sec:league-flaw}

\paragraph{In RAPM.}
Take the model of Equation~\eqref{eq:rapm-model} and add a constant $c$ to the
offence and the defence of every player of one league. Every domestic row is
unchanged: the attacking eleven gain $11c$ and the defending eleven remove
$11c$. A league's level is therefore not identified by its own games. Only
rows with players of two leagues on the pitch constrain it, and the ridge
charges $\lambda c^2$ for every one of the league's player columns. The ridge
wins: every league is centred near zero, and dominating any league looks the
same. The competition-season intercept does not help, because it absorbs the
league's scoring level, not its quality.

The club means show it (Table~\ref{tab:club-rapm}). In the pre-registered fit,
the players of Porto, Benfica and Sporting average between
\ClubRapmPrimaryPortugalLo{} and \ClubRapmPrimaryPortugalHi{} goals per 90 of
RAPM total, against \ClubRapmPrimaryRealMadrid{} for Real Madrid's. The
pre-registered leader and the third-ranked player of the pool by RAPM both
played Portuguese-league seasons in the window.

\paragraph{In the box-score families.}
A goal counts the same in every league. The multiverse has no league-strength
dimension, and its context dimension removes the effect of the player's own
team's Elo, not the strength of his opposition. Julián Quiñones is ranked first
by \QuinonesSpecShare{} of the specifications on two qualifying seasons, both
in the Saudi league.

\paragraph{Why the in-house Elo cannot supply league levels.}
The Elo of Section~\ref{sec:elo} orders teams within a league. Its league
levels are an artefact of how it is fed: domestic-cup games against clubs
outside the covered leagues, leagues that enter late at the starting rating,
and drift with time in the data. Any quantity that pools team Elo across
leagues inherits the artefact. That includes the Elo-partialled level of the
context dimension and M2's partial correlations (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:league-method}

\paragraph{RAPM with league means (A20 (i), A22).}
We follow the league term of \citet{pantuso2021transfer};
\citet{kharrat2020plusminus} likewise estimate one coefficient per league
jointly with the players. Each player's effects are his leagues' mean plus a
deviation, and only the deviation is penalised:
\begin{equation}
  o_i = \tilde o_i + \sum_L s_{iL}\, \mu_L, \qquad
  \delta_i = \tilde\delta_i + \sum_L s_{iL}\, \mu_L ,
  \label{eq:league-effects}
\end{equation}
where $s_{iL}$ is the share of player $i$'s league minutes in the window played
in league $L$, and $\mu_L$ is the league mean (not the competition-season
intercept $\mu_\kappa$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:rapm-model}). With
$n_{\ell a, L} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal L_{\ell a}} s_{iL}$ the number of side $a$'s
players who belong to league $L$, and $N_{\ell a}$ the number of players side
$a$ has on the pitch,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \E[y_{\ell a}] ={}& \sum_{i \in \mathcal L_{\ell a}} \tilde o_i
    - \sum_{j \in \mathcal L_{\ell \bar a}} \tilde\delta_j
    + \sum_L \mu_L \big(n_{\ell a, L} - n_{\ell \bar a, L}\big)
    + \nu \big(N_{\ell a} - N_{\ell \bar a}\big) \\
    &+ h_{\kappa(\ell)}\, \mathbb 1[a = \mathrm H] + \mu_{\kappa(\ell)} ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:league-rapm}
\end{equation}
with the ridge penalty $\lambda \sum_i (\tilde o_i^2 + \tilde\delta_i^2)$ at the
primary $\lambda$, and $\mu_L$, $\nu$, $h_\kappa$ and the intercepts unpenalised.
The ridge now shrinks a player toward his league's mean, not toward zero. With
eleven players a side the league columns sum to zero in every row, so one
league is the reference: $\mu_L = 0$ for the Premier League. A reported rating
is $\beta_i = \tilde o_i + \tilde\delta_i + 2 \sum_L s_{iL} \mu_L$.

A league mean is identified by every row in which the two sides differ in
their league composition: games between clubs of two leagues, domestic games
with a player who has minutes in two leagues, and national-team games. A
league has its own mean if it has at least 100 stint games against clubs of
other covered leagues, or at least 150 players with 450 minutes in it and in
another league; the remaining leagues share one mean. \NLeaguesOwnMean{}
leagues have their own mean, the reference included, and \NLeaguesRest{} share
one. Stint players without minutes in a covered league form one pseudo-league
with its own mean, and the replacement column carries no league term. Standard
errors come from the same 200 game resamples as the primary bootstrap. The
per-season fits keep the pooled means.

The column $N_{\ell a} - N_{\ell \bar a}$ was added by A22, after the first fit
and before any league-adjusted ranking. In \UnequalMinutesShare{} of stint
minutes the sides are unequal, after a dismissal. There the league columns sum
to the difference in players, not to zero, so fixing the reference at zero is a
constraint, and every other league's mean also absorbs the effect of an extra
player. A noise-free fit of a known truth on the real schedule returned each
mean \LeagueNoiseFreeBias{} goals per 90 too high, and the fitted means put
\NLeaguesAboveAsWritten{} leagues above the Premier League and moved with
$\lambda$. With the extra column the same check recovers the truth and the
means are stable from $\lambda/10$ to $10\lambda$. Its coefficient is
\LeagueMen{} goals per 90 per extra player (bootstrap SE \LeagueMenSe{}). The
first fit is kept in the results and not used.

\paragraph{Box-score league factors from players who change league (A20 (ii), A21).}
Translating a rate from one league to another through the players seen in both
is established practice in hockey
\citep{desjardins2005equivalencies,bacon2021nhle}. We estimate it as a
regression. For each metric (goals, non-penalty goals, assists), a conditional
fixed-effects Poisson model \citep{hausman1984count} is fitted on the counts
$Y_{iqL}$ of player $i$ in season $q$ and league $L$, over league matches with
minutes $M_{iqL}$:
\begin{equation}
  \log \E[Y_{iqL}] = \log\frac{M_{iqL}}{90} + \alpha_i + \gamma_L
  + b_1\, a_{iq} + b_2\, a_{iq}^2 + c\, e_{iqL},
  \label{eq:league-factor}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_i$ is a player effect removed by the conditional likelihood,
$\gamma_L$ the league effect with $\gamma_L = 0$ for the Premier League,
$a_{iq}$ the player's age minus 26, and $e_{iqL}$ the minutes-weighted pre-match
Elo of his team in that cell, centred within league-season. A league has its
own $\gamma_L$ with at least 100 players observed in it and in another league;
the others share one. \NLeaguesOwnFactor{} leagues have their own factor and
\NLeaguesPooledFactor{} share one. League-match counts are divided by
$\exp(\hat\gamma_L)$; European-cup and national-team matches are left as they
are. Standard errors come from a bootstrap over players, and $\hat\gamma_L$ is
held fixed across M8's replicates.

Only players observed in two or more leagues identify $\gamma_L$. Effects of a
group identified from those who move between groups are standard in labour
economics \citep{abowd1999wage}, and are known to be noisy when movers are few
\citep{andrews2008mobility}. A factor is the typical mover's change in rate, so
it mixes the league's difficulty with the mover's change of team. Players who
arrive in the Premier League usually leave a dominant club for a less dominant
one, and those who leave it do the reverse, which overstates its difficulty.
The team term $c\, e_{iqL}$ was added for that reason (A21), after the factors
without it had been estimated and before any league-adjusted ranking existed.
Within-league Elo differences are usable (Section~\ref{sec:elo}). The team term
enters only the estimation of $\gamma_L$: counts are divided by the league
factor alone, so a player is not adjusted for his own team. The ranking uses
the factors with the team term; those without it are reported beside them, with
the ranking they give, as a sensitivity.

\paragraph{The rest of the adjustment (A20 (iii) to (vi)).}
On-pitch goal difference in league matches is shifted by $22\,\hat\mu_L$ per 90:
against average Premier League opposition, a team of league $L$ would meet
eleven players each better by $2\mu_L$ in total effect. In the context
dimension, team Elo is centred within competition-season. M5 and M8 are then
rerun exactly as the primary: the same factorial, the unmeasured shares of the
primary M4, the same $\omega$, $U$, $B$ and seeds. M4, M6 and M7 are not rerun.

A separate variant fills one coverage hole. The MLS seasons before 2025 of
pool players come from the American Soccer Analysis interface
\citep{asa2026api}: \NMlsFillRows{} player-seasons of \NMlsFillPlayers{}
players, with minutes, goals, penalty goals and primary assists. The source
has no line-ups, so these seasons have no stints: they take the player's
pooled RAPM and the group-season mean of on-pitch goal difference, the
existing rule for rows without stints. Minutes are rescaled to 90 per match,
because the source counts stoppage time (A23 (iii)). The Saudi league before
2024/25 remains a hole: we found no source with minutes and line-ups under
terms we could use.

\paragraph{Validation on synthetic leagues (A20 (vii), A21).}
Both estimators had to recover planted league levels before they were used.
% The numbers of this paragraph are those recorded in the comments of
% tests/players/test_rapm.py (league means) and tests/players/test_league_factors.py
% (factors); they come from synthetic data, not from results/players/.
For the league means, on four synthetic leagues linked by a cup and by 3\% of
players changing league each season, the slope of the estimated on the planted
means averaged 0.97 over 16 seeds (SD 0.13), and the correlation of the
estimated with the true total effect, players of all linked leagues pooled,
was higher with the league columns than without them in each of the 16. With
every planted mean at zero the estimated means stayed near zero and the
ranking was almost unchanged. A fifth league with ten movers and no cup game had a
bootstrap standard deviation 6 to 21 times that of a linked league, which is
why weakly linked leagues are pooled.

For the factors, with movers drawn at random the bias of $\hat\gamma$ was at
most 0.01 in absolute value and the 90\% interval covered the truth in 0.86 to
0.90 of datasets, slightly under nominal. When movers also change team
strength with league, the model without the team term was biased upward by
0.11 to 0.15, and the team term reduced the bias to 0.02 or less. Two biases
remain. When moves are selected on form, the factor of the league the movers
leave is biased by about 0.08, and neither of our two checks (a dummy for the
season before a move, and dropping that season) removes it; in simulation the
two bracket the truth. And the age terms matter: without them, a league that
players join late in their careers looks harder than it is. On the real data,
the largest shift of a goal factor of a league that holds a pool player-season
is \FactorShiftDummyMaxSe{} standard errors with the dummy and
\FactorShiftDroppedMaxSe{} without the season before a move.

\LeagueGapPortugal{} goals per 90 below an average Premier League team. With
the league means the clubs separate: the players of Porto, Benfica and
Sporting now average between \ClubRapmLeaguePortugalLo{} and
\ClubRapmLeaguePortugalHi{}, against \ClubRapmLeagueRealMadrid{} for Real
Madrid's (Table~\ref{tab:club-rapm}). Over all players with their own columns,
the rank correlation between the two RAPM totals is \LeagueRapmSpearmanAll{};
within the pool, most of whose players are in the five largest leagues, it is
\LeagueRapmSpearmanPool{}.

The box-score factors are in Table~\ref{tab:league-factors}. A goal in La Liga
counts \FactorGoalsSpain{} of a Premier League goal, a goal in the Bundesliga
\FactorGoalsGermany{}, a goal in the Portuguese league \FactorGoalsPortugal{}
and a goal in MLS \FactorGoalsMls{}. Without the team term every factor is
lower (the Portuguese goal factor is \FactorGoalsPortugalPlain{}), which is the
direction A21 expected. The team term itself is clear: a team 100 Elo points
above its league-season mean raises a player's expected goals by a log rate of
\FactorTeamEloGoals{}.

Several of these estimates look wrong, and we say so plainly.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Brazil, Saudi Arabia and MLS.} Their league means and factors
    rest on a handful of Club World Cup games and on moves in one direction,
    mostly of older players who leave Europe. Brazil's league mean is level
    with those of Serie A and La Liga, and the means of MLS and the Saudi
    league are above the Portuguese league's. The Saudi goal factor,
    \FactorGoalsSaudi{}, is level with the Bundesliga's. These three leagues
    have the largest standard errors of all the leagues with their own mean.
    RAPM has no age curve, so a player who moves down late in his career is
    worse on arrival than his window average, which makes the league he
    joins look stronger than it is; the factors depend on the age curve being
    right for ageing stars.
  \item \emph{Assists.} Assist factors differ between leagues of similar
    level: \FactorAssistsSpain{} for La Liga, \FactorAssistsItaly{} for Serie A
    and \FactorAssistsGermany{} for the Bundesliga. We have no explanation.
  \item \emph{Penalties.} Goals are deflated more than non-penalty goals in
    every league. The goal factor therefore also carries who takes the
    penalties after a move, which is a matter of role, not of league
    difficulty.
  \item \emph{The reference league.} The Premier League gains through every
    channel at once: the league term of RAPM, the shift of on-pitch goal
    difference, and the three factors. If its level is overstated, for
    instance because the team term corrects the movers' change of team only in
    part, all three err in the same direction, in favour of its players.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Validation of the league levels}
\label{sec:league-validation}

The league means are compared, descriptively, with levels that use no player
and no mover (A23 (i)). The first is a least-squares rating of clubs on results
alone: goal difference equals home rating minus away rating plus a home term,
fitted on the club games between clubs of covered leagues, with one rating per
club over the window. A league's level is the mean rating of its club-seasons
minus the Premier League's, in goals per game, the scale of $22\,\hat\mu_L$.
A league is rated if it has at least 100 games against clubs of other covered
leagues. The second is the raw goal difference in the league's games against
Premier League clubs. The third is UEFA's association coefficient
\citep{uefa2026coefficients}, which counts results in European competition
only and exists only for UEFA's members.

Over the \ValNLeaguesClub{} leagues with both values, the correlation between
$22\,\hat\mu_L$ and the club-results level is \ValPearsonClub{} (Spearman
\ValSpearmanClub{}), and the slope of the second on the first, through the
reference, is \ValSlopeClub{}. For most leagues the two agree within their
standard errors, the Bundesliga, the Portuguese league and the Eredivisie among
them. They differ by two standard errors or more for \ValNTwoSeClub{} leagues.
The largest difference is for \ValMaxGapLeague{}, which the league mean puts
\ValMaxGapRapm{} goals per 90 below the Premier League at team level and the
club results \ValMaxGapClub{}; the Scottish and Swedish leagues differ in the
same direction. La Liga differs in the other direction: the league mean puts
it \ValGapSpainRapm{} below the Premier League, the club results
\ValGapSpainClub{}. If the club results are right, the league term is too
harsh on La Liga's players, who are many in the pool, and too kind to players
of a few smaller leagues, who are not.

With UEFA's coefficient, over \ValNLeaguesUefa{} leagues, the Spearman
correlation of the league means is \ValSpearmanUefa{}, against
\ValSpearmanClubUefa{} for the club-results levels. The league means agree
less with UEFA's ordering than the club results do, which is expected: both of
those use results only, and the league means also use players who move.

The same club data give a check of the goal factors. A Poisson model of goals
with an attack and a defence per club gives, for each league, what a goal
against its defences is worth in goals against Premier League defences. Over
\ValNFactorGoals{} leagues these multipliers and the movers' goal factors
correlate at \ValPearsonFactorGoals{} (Spearman \ValSpearmanFactorGoals{}), and
they differ by two standard errors or more for \ValNTwoSeFactorGoals{} leagues;
for non-penalty goals, for \ValNTwoSeFactorNpGoals{}. Where they differ, the
movers' factor is the higher one: it discounts the league less than club
results would. The two are not the same quantity, since a mover also changes
team-mates, but the direction is the one the caveats of
Section~\ref{sec:league-levels} lead one to expect.

\subsection{Why the impact view ranks as it does}
\label{sec:league-onoff}

The league adjustment leaves \TopPlayerName{} near the top of the impact view.
To see what RAPM rewards, Table~\ref{tab:on-off} gives, for pool players, their
clubs' goal difference per 90 with the player on the pitch and off it, over
club matches with stints. This is a description, not an estimate of anything.

\TopPlayerName{}'s clubs score \OnOffLeaderOn{} goals per 90 more than they
concede with him on the pitch and \OnOffLeaderOff{} without him, a difference
of \OnOffLeaderDiff{}. He played \OnOffLeaderShare{} of his clubs' minutes, so
the comparison rests on many minutes off. For Mohamed Salah the difference is
\OnOffSalahDiff{}, with \OnOffSalahShare{} of his club's minutes played and
therefore few minutes off. For Lionel Messi it is \OnOffMessiDiff{}.

RAPM is a regularised version of this contrast. It can credit a player only
through the difference between the stints he plays and the stints he does
not, given who else is on the pitch. For a player who rarely leaves the pitch
that difference is poorly determined, and the ridge shares his team's results
among its regulars: RAPM cannot separate an ever-present star from his team.
For a player with many minutes off, the difference is well determined, but it
is not all his doing. It also reflects when he was absent: an injury during a
poor spell, a rotation against weaker opposition, or a different squad in a
different season all enter the contrast. Across the outfield pool, the rank
correlation of pooled RAPM total with on-minus-off goal difference is
\OnOffSpearmanDiff{}, and with the share of the team's minutes played
\OnOffSpearmanShare{}. The second is positive: within the pool, players who
play more of their team's minutes tend to have a higher RAPM, so RAPM does not
simply penalise regulars. The point concerns what it can identify, player by
player.

This is the teammate contamination that post-hoc analysis (f) of
Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} adds to the mixture, and A23 (ii) applies the same
term to the league-adjusted run. With the attribution factors of independent
planting, \LeagueLeakIndepTopName{} still has the highest $P(\#1)$, at
\LeagueLeakIndepTopPFirst{}; \LeagueLeakIndepSecondName{} is second, at
\LeagueLeakIndepSecondPFirst{}; and \TopPlayerName{} falls to
\LeagueLeakIndepLeaderPFirst{}. The share of decided pairs falls to
\LeagueLeakIndepSharePairsDecided{}. With the attribution factors of the real
teammate structure, which imply less contamination, the leader's $P(\#1)$ is
\LeagueLeakAssortTopPFirst{}, \TopPlayerName{}'s is
\LeagueLeakAssortLeaderPFirst{}, and \LeagueLeakAssortSharePairsDecided{} of
pairs are decided. The players who lose most are those who lead on RAPM; the
players who lead on box scores hardly move.

\section{Other post-hoc and robustness analyses}
\label{sec:r-posthoc}

Every analysis in this section was added after the primary results were
seen, and each was prompted by them. Each is exploratory, and none is used to
decide a pre-registered verdict. They concern the pre-registered analysis,
not the league-adjusted one, unless stated. Table~\ref{tab:robustness} gives the numbers
of (a), (b), (c) and (e), and Table~\ref{tab:leakage} those of (f).

\paragraph{(a) H3 under assortative planting.}
\emph{Motivation.} On the real data, $R^2_{\text{true}}$ exceeds 1 for
centre-forwards and attacking midfielders, which suggests that the attribution
factor, computed with effects planted independently of who plays with whom,
understates attribution when quality clusters on teams. \emph{Analysis.} We
reran the A9 parametric bootstrap with planted effects equal to the real pooled
RAPM, rescaled to the calibrated standard deviations, so that planted quality
keeps the real teammate structure. \emph{Reading.} Under this planting the
full-data attribution factor of RAPM total lies between \AssortAttrLo{} and
\AssortAttrHi{} across outfield groups, against \AttrFullTotal{} with
independent effects. That explains the anomaly: with the larger factor, RAPM's
reliability is higher and the corrected $R^2$ lower. But this planting
over-reproduces the anomaly. The mean simulated $R^2_{\text{true}}$ exceeds 1
in every outfield group, whereas the real data show it only for centre-forwards
and attacking midfielders. Rescaled RAPM carries the ridge's own leakage, so it
plants more teammate structure than the truth has. The truth likely lies
between the two plantings.

Under this planting the planted contrast averages \AssortPlanted{}, and the
raw estimate is biased upward by \AssortMeanErrorRaw{} on average. A pool is
clipped in \AssortSimClipped{} of the simulations, and the simulated raw
contrast reaches the real one in \AssortSimReachReal{} of them: this planting
produces estimates as extreme as the real one, which the independent planting
of A9 does not. The 90\% basic interval is [\AssortRawLo, \AssortRawHi] for
the raw contrast and [\AssortClippedLo, \AssortClippedHi] for the clipped one.
M4's own player-bootstrap interval covers the planted contrast in only
\AssortCoverageRaw{} of simulations for the raw contrast and in
\AssortCoverageClipped{} for the clipped one, so it should not be trusted
under this planting. Both basic intervals exclude zero: H3's direction holds
under both plantings, and its size does not.

\paragraph{(b) M8 with bias-corrected unmeasured shares.}
\emph{Motivation.} If the planting of (a) were the truth, M4 would understate
the unmeasured share in almost every group, and M8's unmeasured component
would be too small. \emph{Analysis.} We subtract each group's mean simulated
error under (a) from its real raw share, clip the result to $[0,1]$, and rerun
M8 with these shares. They are
\BiasShareCF{} for centre-forwards and between \BiasShareOtherLo{} and 1 for the
other outfield groups. This is an extreme bound, because (a) over-reproduces
the anomaly. \emph{Reading.} The share of decided pairs falls to
\BiasSharePairsDecided{}, and the unmeasured term becomes the largest part of
the leader's rank variance (\BiasTopPlayerVarUnm{}). The player with the highest
$P(\#1)$ and the verdicts of H1, H2, H4 and H5 are unchanged. If statistics see
little of on-pitch impact in every group, the ranking is even less decided than
the primary analysis says, but its leader and the verdicts of H1, H2, H4 and H5
stand.

\paragraph{(c) M8 restricted to European leagues.}
\emph{Motivation.} Julián Quiñones is ranked first by \QuinonesSpecShare{} of
the specifications on the strength of his two qualifying seasons, both in the
Saudi league (2024/25 and 2025/26), mostly under the mean and recency
aggregations. The
factorial has no league-strength dimension, and its context dimension partials
out the player's own team's Elo, not the strength of the opposition, and that
Elo does not compare leagues (Section~\ref{sec:elo}). \emph{Analysis.} We reran M5 and M8 after
dropping the league seasons of leagues outside UEFA; Champions League and
national-team matches are kept, and RAPM is unchanged. \NPoolLostSeasons{} pool
players lose at least one qualifying season: \EuropeLostPlayers{}. Quiñones keeps
no qualifying season and falls to the bottom of the ranking. \emph{Reading.}
The player with the highest $P(\#1)$ and every verdict of M8 are unchanged.
The primary result is not driven by leagues outside Europe, but its second
place was. This restriction is blunt; the league-adjusted analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:league} replaces it with estimated league levels.

\paragraph{(d) H7 with continental and international knockouts only.}
\emph{Motivation.} This sensitivity is post hoc and prompted by the data. After
the primary H7 result, we noted that the knockout flag counts domestic cups,
super cups and qualifying rounds, often against weaker sides, so persistence
might reflect who scores against weak cup opponents rather than who rises in
big games. \emph{Analysis.} We keep only the knockout games of the Champions
League, the Europa League, the Conference League, the Club World Cup and
national-team tournaments, and drop the other knockout games from the baseline
too. \emph{Reading.} \NPlayersClutchMajor{} players qualify, and $r$ =
\ClutchMajorR{} [\ClutchMajorRLo, \ClutchMajorRHi], with one-sided p-value
\ClutchMajorP{}. The small persistence of the primary analysis does not appear
in big games. This test has even less power than the primary one, so it is not
evidence against a big-game trait; it is only no evidence for one.

\paragraph{(e) M6 with curated team honours.}
\emph{Motivation.} The team-success block is measured with gaps
(Section~\ref{sec:r-m6}) that bias it down, toward refuting H6.
\emph{Analysis.} We refit M6 with team honours curated from published records,
each fact checked against two sources (\texttt{paper-unmeasured/data/team\_honours\_sources.md}):
official league champions, including titles decided by play-offs or a cup final
(MLS); the 2025/26 Champions League final; and the international tournaments
missing from the data (Copa Am\'erica 2021, the 2021 and 2023 Africa Cups of
Nations, the UEFA Nations League finals of 2021, 2023 and 2025, the 2025
CONCACAF Nations League and Gold Cup, and the knockout rounds of the 2026 World
Cup). A player counts for a tournament only if he is in the winning squad. The
curation changes \CuratedNChanged{} team-success indicators, every one from 0
to 1. It adds honours only: the matches of the missing tournaments stay absent
from the statistics block. \emph{Reading.} The OLS Shapley share of team success rises to
\CuratedShapleyTeamOls{} and that of individual statistics is
\CuratedShapleyStatsOls{}; team success exceeds statistics in
\CuratedTeamAboveStatsBoot{} of the bootstrap replicates, and H6 with curated
honours is \CuratedVerdictHSix{} (Table~\ref{tab:recognition-curated}). The
data gaps therefore narrow the gap between the two blocks without reversing
it. The verdict is nonetheless fragile: under the per-90 reading of the
statistics block (itself post hoc, Section~\ref{sec:r-m6}), the curated shares
are \CuratedPerNinetyTeamOls{} for team success and \CuratedPerNinetyStatsOls{}
for statistics, so which block leads depends on how the statistics are
counted.

\paragraph{(f) M8 with RAPM leakage propagated.}
\emph{Motivation.} The leader's lead rests on RAPM, and RAPM credits part of
each player's stable estimate to his usual teammates and opponents
(Section~\ref{sec:r-m3}). That leakage is stable across game resamples, so the
primary mixture's sampling layer does not contain it, and the impact family
gets no unmeasured draw: the primary analysis treats RAPM's attribution as
certain. \emph{Analysis.} Within group $g$, write the stable part of a RAPM
score as $S = \text{own} + \text{leak}$, the two independent, with the
attribution factor $A_g$ as own's share of $\Var(S)$. Ranking by own given $S$,
$\E[\text{own} \mid S] = A_g S$, which keeps the order, and
$\Var(\text{own} \mid S) = A_g (1 - A_g) \Var(S)$; in score units the equivalent
Gaussian draw has variance $(1 - A_g)\Var(S)/A_g$, with
$\Var(S) = \rho^{\mathrm{SH}}_g \widehat{\Var}_g(x_{sb})$. This draw is added
for the on-pitch share $1 - \omega_s$ of each specification:
\begin{equation}
  \tau^2_{sb,i} = \widehat{\Var}_{g}(x_{sb}) \left[
  \frac{\omega_s \tilde\sigma_{g}}{1 - \omega_s \tilde\sigma_{g}}
  + (1 - \omega_s)\, \lambda_g \right],
  \qquad
  \lambda_g = \rho^{\mathrm{SH}}_g \, \frac{1 - A_g}{A_g},
  \label{eq:tau-leak}
\end{equation}
with $g = g(i)$. On-pitch goal difference counts with the on-pitch part, as in
$\omega_s$; it is noisier than RAPM, so the term is conservative for the box+GD
and hybrid families. We run M8 twice: with the attribution factors of
independent planting (M3) and with those of the assortative planting of (a),
which are larger and give less leakage. \emph{Reading.} With the
independent-planting factors, $\lambda_g$ lies between \LeakLambdaIndepLo{} and
\LeakLambdaIndepHi{} across outfield groups: the added draw is of the same
order as the visible spread of RAPM. The pre-registered leader's $P(\#1)$ falls
from \TopPlayerPFirst{} to \LeakIndepLeaderPFirst{}, and the player with the
highest $P(\#1)$ becomes \LeakIndepTopName{}, at \LeakIndepTopPFirst{}. He leads
by default: his place comes from the box-score families, which carry no
leakage term, and from two Saudi league seasons. \LeakIndepSharePairsDecided{}
of outfield pairs are decided. With the assortative factors, $\lambda_g$ lies
between \LeakLambdaAssortLo{} and \LeakLambdaAssortHi{}; the leader is
\LeakAssortTopName{}, at \LeakAssortTopPFirst{}, and
\LeakAssortSharePairsDecided{} of pairs are decided
(Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). In both runs the statistics behind H1, H2, H4 and H5
stay on the same side of their thresholds. The pre-registered lead therefore
depends on treating RAPM's attribution as certain. Once it is not, the lead
shrinks, or passes to a player whose own place does not survive the league
adjustment of Section~\ref{sec:league}.

\section{Who was the best player of the 2020s?}
\label{sec:r-best}

Public match data cannot name him. The paper gives two answers, and they are
not on the same footing.

\paragraph{The pre-registered answer.}
The most likely number one is \TopPlayerName{}, with $P(\#1)$ =
\TopPlayerPFirst{}, a median rank of \TopPlayerMedianRank{} and a 90\% rank
interval from \TopPlayerRankLo{} to \TopPlayerRankHi{}. His lead rests on one
kind of evidence. He has the highest pooled RAPM of the outfield pool, and the
two families that contain RAPM rank him first far more often than the
box-score families: the impact family \TopFirstImpact{} of the time, the box
family \TopFirstBox{}. The second most likely number one,
\SecondPlayerName{}, owes his place to two Saudi league seasons that the
pre-registered multiverse has no way to discount. This is the answer that
decides the hypotheses. It has a flaw we can demonstrate: it does not adjust
for league strength.

\paragraph{The league-adjusted answer (post hoc).}
With league means in RAPM and league factors in the box score, the most likely
number one is \LeagueTopName{}, with $P(\#1)$ = \LeagueTopPFirst{}, a median
rank of \LeagueTopMedianRank{} and a 90\% rank interval from \LeagueTopRankLo{}
to \LeagueTopRankHi{}. \TopPlayerName{} is second, at \LeagueSecondPFirst{}. By
median rank, \LeagueMedianTopThree{} lead (Table~\ref{tab:league-ranking}). This
answer corrects the flaw, but it was built after the results were known, its
league levels are poorly determined outside Europe, and it favours the
reference league through every channel at once
(Section~\ref{sec:league-levels}).

\paragraph{What the two answers share.}
In neither is any player a likely number one, and in neither are more than a
small share of pairwise orderings decided. Across every variant we ran, the
highest $P(\#1)$ of any player is \AllVariantsMaxPFirst{}, and the largest share
of decided outfield pairs is \AllVariantsMaxDecided{}. Both answers also depend
on how far one trusts RAPM's division of goals among teammates. When that
uncertainty is added, the players who lead on RAPM lose probability. In the
pre-registered mixture, under the larger of the two leakage estimates,
\TopPlayerName{} falls to \LeakIndepLeaderPFirst{} and is no longer first
(Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (f)). In the league-adjusted mixture
\LeagueTopName{}, whose lead rests mostly on box scores, stays first under both
estimates
(Section~\ref{sec:league-onoff}). A reader who trusts RAPM's attribution will
rank \TopPlayerName{} first or second; a reader who trusts box scores will rank
prolific scorers first; the Ballon d'Or voters ranked \TopPlayerName{} low.

The honest answer is therefore a distribution, not a name: a handful of
players each have a modest chance of being first, the ordering among the
leaders is undecided, and much of what moves it is the choice of what to
measure and of how to compare leagues. In the recovery study, two of our
estimators recover a planted best player in a minority of simulated leagues,
so this outcome is what data of this size would lead one to expect.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Coverage holes by design.}
The window ends on 2026-08-31, and the source has holes inside it.
\NLeaguesLateStart{} of the \NLeaguesPanel{} first-tier leagues enter only in
2024/25 or later; whole national-team tournaments are absent, as are
qualifiers, the Nations League and friendlies; the 2026 Champions League and
Europa League finals are missing, and the 2026 World Cup stops after its group
stage. A player's matches outside covered competitions are missing from his
record: from his statistics, his minutes and his RAPM, not only from his
team's honours. \NPoolStructuralHole{} of the \NPool{} pool players have a
season whose club league is absent or partly absent. Missing matches lower a
player's minutes and can move him below the 900- or 1,800-minute thresholds.
StatsBomb-based estimates (M7a) come from tournaments plus three clubs, not
from leagues. A post-hoc variant fills the MLS seasons before 2025 of pool
players from another source (Section~\ref{sec:league-method}); nothing fills
the others.

\paragraph{No league-strength adjustment in the pre-registered analysis.}
This is the main flaw of the pre-registered analysis, and we found it after
the results (Section~\ref{sec:league}). Its RAPM centres every league near
zero, its box-score families count a goal the same in every league, and its
multiverse has no league-strength dimension. A strong season in a weak league
can therefore rank very high. The hypotheses were decided by this analysis all
the same, as pre-registered; the league-adjusted results are reported beside
it.

\paragraph{Elo pooled across leagues.}
The in-house Elo orders teams within a league and does not compare leagues
(Section~\ref{sec:elo}). Two parts of the pre-registered analysis pool team Elo
across leagues anyway: the Elo-partialled level of M5's context dimension, and
the partial correlations of M2. Both therefore remove a mixture of real team
strength and league artefact. The league-adjusted analysis centres team Elo
within competition-season; M2 was not rerun. The national-team pool was
calibrated on few games, and its home advantage sits at the edge of the search
grid.

\paragraph{Limits of the league adjustment.}
The adjustment is post hoc. It gives one level per league for the whole
window, so a league that changes, as the Saudi league did after 2023, gets
its average. It gives the same shift to every player of a league, and the same
to offence and defence. The levels of Brazil, MLS and the Saudi league rest on
a handful of Club World Cup games and on one-way moves of older players, and
they look too high; no club-results rating can check them, for lack of games.
Where a check exists, it agrees for most leagues, but it puts La Liga closer
to the Premier League than the league mean does
(Section~\ref{sec:league-validation}), and many pool players are in La Liga. The box-score factors are the typical mover's change in
rate, so they mix league difficulty with the mover's change of team and role;
the team term corrects this only in part, and selection of moves on form
biases them in a way our checks bracket but do not remove. The assist factors
differ between leagues of similar level for reasons we cannot explain. The
Premier League, as the reference, gains through the league term of RAPM, the
shift of goal difference and the factors at once. M4, M6 and M7 were not rerun,
so the league-adjusted mixture uses the unmeasured shares of the
pre-registered M4, and nothing in it corrects the recognition model. The MLS
fill has no line-ups, and the Saudi league before 2024/25 remains a hole.

\paragraph{Teammate leakage.}
Ridge RAPM spreads a player's effect over his usual teammates and opponents.
The attribution factor (Equation~\eqref{eq:attribution}) removes the stable
part of that leak from the reliability, but it is computed on planted effects
that are independent of who plays with whom. Real lineups are not: good players
tend to play together. When planted quality keeps the real teammate structure,
the factor rises from \AttrFullTotal{} to between \AssortAttrLo{} and
\AssortAttrHi{} (Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (a)). The consequence is visible in
M4: $R^2_{\text{true}}$ exceeds 1 for centre-forwards and attacking
midfielders, so their unmeasured share is clipped at zero and not resolved,
and the defenders' share is equally sensitive to the factor. The size of the H3
contrast is therefore uncertain beyond its interval. The primary synthesis
also treats RAPM's attribution as certain; Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (f)
relaxes that, and the pre-registered leader does not keep his place under the
larger of the two leakage estimates. More generally, RAPM credits a player
through the contrast between the minutes he plays and those he does not, so
it cannot separate an ever-present player from his team
(Section~\ref{sec:league-onoff}). RAPM's additivity ignores chemistry, although the evidence that
chemistry matters beyond team strength is mixed
\citep{mukherjee2019shared,lecoz2025shared}.

\paragraph{What RAPM is.}
RAPM measures effects on goals while the player is on the pitch. Goals are a
sparse response \citep{kharrat2020plusminus}, and the primary analysis has no
shot-quality response for every league. Effects that act when the player is off
the pitch, or through teammates in later seasons, are outside both RAPM and the
statistics, so they are outside the unmeasured share too. The per-season
estimates are pooled RAPM by season, close to a career constant: they barely
let an impact-family score change with the season, and in M6 they bring
information from later seasons into the vote's season.

\paragraph{Goalkeepers.}
No public statistic in the primary data measures shot-stopping. M4's estimated
unmeasured share for goalkeepers is high, but its interval runs from
\ShareGKLo{} to \ShareGKHi{}. Goalkeepers are ranked separately, on RAPM and on
box metrics that say little about their job; their box metrics are shrunk
almost entirely to the goalkeeper mean by a post-hoc choice of constant. That
ranking is the least informative in the paper.

\paragraph{The statistics chosen by A11.}
M4's statistics are five per-90 box-score rates: non-penalty goals, penalty
goals, assists, yellow cards and red cards. They were chosen because they exist
for every match of the panel, not because they are the best public statistics.
The unmeasured share is therefore the share unseen by these five. It mixes what
no public number reaches with what richer public event data could reach, and it
is an upper bound on the share a superset of these statistics would leave.

\paragraph{Team honours.}
The team-success block of M6 is computed from the games. It misses league
titles decided by play-offs and the honours of tournaments absent from the
data, and the 2026 World Cup stops at its group stage. These gaps bias the team
share down, toward refuting H6; Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (e) measures how
much. The statistics block, built from season totals and minutes, also carries
part of team success through deep runs, and it lacks the matches of the absent
tournaments.

\paragraph{H7's design.}
At the reliabilities observed, the over-performance score is almost pure noise,
and the expected observed correlation for a perfect true correlation is below
the refutation threshold of 0.2. H7 could not have been refuted, whatever the
truth, and the A5 power rule makes it inconclusive. The observed correlation
is nonetheless positive and significant, and corrected for attenuation it
leans toward a persistent trait, with a very wide interval. A useful test of a
big-game trait needs many more knockout minutes per player than these periods
provide, or a less noisy measure of performance.

\paragraph{The team share in M2.}
ICC(1) pools every outfield position, so it understates how much of a
position's output is team; and its club-bootstrap intervals are approximate
(A17).

\paragraph{The V1-like weights.}
V1's weights cannot be recovered. The V1-like weights of M2 and M5 are a
stylised imitation of its emphasis, chosen by us; conclusions about them are
conclusions about that imitation, not about V1.

\paragraph{The Ballon d'Or is recognition, not truth.}
M6 measures what the vote rewards, not who was best. Voters see things our
data do not, and our data see things voters ignore. The recognition view in M8
is one view among three, not a validation target.

\paragraph{The synthesis.}
M4's share enters M8 at its point estimate, so its own uncertainty is not
propagated; Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} (b) gives an extreme bound instead.
RAPM's teammate leakage is not propagated either; Section~\ref{sec:r-posthoc}
(f) adds it. If the pool's visible spread understates the population's, the
scale $\tau$ is too small, which is conservative for H1: it understates rank
uncertainty. The match bootstrap of ranks can be inconsistent near ties
\citep{hall2009bootstrap}; the rank confidence sets of \citet{mogstad2024ranks}
are reported beside it at one specification.

\paragraph{Post-hoc analyses.}
The analyses of Sections~\ref{sec:league} and~\ref{sec:r-posthoc} were
prompted by the results, and they are not a neutral sample of the checks one
could run. Most probe choices that the results made salient: the league
adjustment was prompted by an implausible second place and by club means that
did not separate leagues, and (f) is aimed at the measure on which the
pre-registered leader's lead rests. A reader should weigh the league-adjusted
answer as a correction made with the answer in view.

\paragraph{Other choices.}
Elo applies home advantage to neutral-venue tournament games, because the
source has no venue flag; Elo enters only M2, M5's context dimension and a
robustness fit. Name linking between sources can err; medium-confidence pool
matches are listed. The taxonomy's sizes come from the literature, mostly from
abstracts, and many cells have no verified size. Several analysis choices were
made after the data were seen; each is labelled where it applies, and those
that bear on a hypothesis or on a reported view are recorded as amendments A16
to A23.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Who was the best footballer of 2020--2026? On public match data, the answer is
a distribution, and it depends on an analysis choice we got wrong the first
time. The pre-registered analysis makes \TopPlayerName{} the most likely number
one, at \TopPlayerPFirst{}. It does not adjust for league strength, a flaw we
found after its results. A post-hoc analysis that adds league means to RAPM
and league factors to the box score makes \LeagueTopName{} the most likely
number one, at \LeagueTopPFirst{}, with \TopPlayerName{} second. We report both
and claim neither as the answer: the first is flawed, and the second was built
with the first in view and rests on league levels that are weak outside
Europe.

What the two analyses share is the paper's result. In every variant we ran,
no player has a probability above \AllVariantsMaxPFirst{} of being the best,
at most \AllVariantsMaxDecided{} of the pool's pairwise orderings are decided,
and almost none among the leaders. Much of what moves a leading player's rank
is the definition of ``best'', and within it the choice of what is measured:
box scores, on-pitch goal difference or adjusted plus-minus. On average over
the leaders, sampling noise comes second, narrowly; for several of them
individually it is as large or larger. A lead that rests on RAPM also depends
on treating its division of goals among teammates as certain; when that
uncertainty is added, such leads shrink.

The unmeasured remainder is large where statistics have least to count. For
centre-backs and defensive midfielders, the box score misses most of their
on-pitch impact (\ShareHigh{} pooled); for attackers our correction
over-corrects and cannot resolve the share. Under an extreme bound, in which
quality is assumed to cluster on teams as strongly as in the real RAPM, the
remainder is large almost everywhere, and the ranking is even less decided.
M4 measures the size of the remainder, not its content. It may include what
the tracking-only rows of the taxonomy describe: off-ball runs, space
creation, positional defending and pressing; it may also include leakage,
league effects and noise that our corrections miss.

In simulated leagues on the real schedule, pooled RAPM recovers a planted best
player in \RecoveryRapmFirst{} of leagues and a box-score composite in
\RecoveryBoxFirst{}, so data of this size rarely single out the best player even
when one exists. Until richer data are public, a ranking of the best player of
the 2020s should be read as a statement about the definition chosen, and about
how leagues are compared, more than about the players.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

The whole paper is rebuilt from public sources by three make targets.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{make players-data} downloads Transfermarkt and StatsBomb data
    (cached, never re-fetched) and builds the processed tables: games,
    appearances, stints, Elo, links and the panel.
  \item \texttt{make players-results} runs every analysis (M1 to M9) with the
    master seed 20261114 (A12). It takes hours.
  \item \texttt{make paper2} writes the macros, tables and figures into
    \path{paper-unmeasured/generated/} from \path{results/players/}, compiles
    this document, and copies the PDF to
    \path{The-Unmeasured-Remainder.pdf} at the repository root.
\end{itemize}

Raw downloads live under \path{data/players/raw/<source>/}, each source with a
\path{manifest.json} that records the URL, the retrieval time and the SHA-256
of every file. Processed tables are \path{data/players/processed/<name>.csv.gz},
results \path{results/players/<module>/}. Raw and processed data are not
redistributed (Appendix~\ref{app:licences}); the code reproduces them.

Every number in this document is a macro. Its default, declared in
\path{paper-unmeasured/macros-defaults.tex} with the quantity it stands for
and the module that produces it, renders as ``[pending]''; \texttt{make paper2}
redefines it from a results file. Every table and figure that depends on
results is read from \path{generated/}. A slot that renders as pending has
not been produced by a run.

The post-hoc league-strength-adjusted analysis is run by
\path{scripts/players/run_league.py} (league means and factors) and by the
league stages of \path{scripts/players/run_ranking.py}; the MLS fill is fetched
by \path{scripts/players/acquire_asa.py}.

Each analysis module is pure and tested on synthetic data with a planted
truth, and the repository's checks (\texttt{ruff}, \texttt{mypy --strict},
\texttt{pytest}) run with \texttt{make verify}.
